# Making Hard Look Easy

*On the process nobody sees, the value nobody measures, and why sometimes playing in the subway is fine*

This week, doing my team's mid-year reviews, I had to talk about someone several people were unhappy with. Not with their results, exactly. With something vaguer. That feeling a team gets that someone isn't quite contributing what they should, even though if you sit down and look at the numbers, the numbers are fine. Better than fine.

And while we talked it over, something clicked. The team's dissatisfaction didn't come from this person's results. It came from not seeing their process. This person solves things quietly, without making noise, without showing the work. They deliver and move on. And because nobody sees how hard the thing they deliver is, the team assumes it's easy, that anyone could do it, that there's no great merit in it. The dissatisfaction wasn't a judgment on quality. It was a symptom of not knowing. They didn't know what it cost, so they didn't value it.

To explain it, a metaphor came out of me. Imagine you buy two blankets. One at a big-box store, straight out of a factory, flawless, cheap, identical to ten thousand others. The other at a craft fair, hand-woven by someone who spent weeks on it. Both keep you equally warm. They do exactly the same job. If all you care about is not being cold, they're identical, and I'd even say the factory one is better because it's cheaper. But the handmade one costs brutally more. Not because it's warmer. Because someone made it by hand, with time, with intention. That difference, invisible in the function, is everything when we're talking about value.

This person's problem was that they wove by hand and delivered without mentioning they'd woven by hand. The team got the blanket and saw a factory one. Not out of bad faith. Because they had no way of seeing the weeks of weaving. And that's the whole subject of this essay, which isn't about that person or about me, but about something that happens to everyone who makes the hard look easy: people don't value what they can't see, and the thing done well is precisely the thing that's least visible.

### I. Thirty seconds and forty years

There's a story about Picasso, one of those that probably didn't happen exactly this way but is too good not to tell. Picasso is at a café, or in a park, depending on who's telling it. A woman recognizes him and asks him to draw something on a napkin. Picasso does it in thirty seconds, hands it to her, and asks for an absurd amount of money. The woman is offended. How can he charge that for something that took him thirty seconds? And Picasso, without blinking, says: it didn't take me thirty seconds. It took me forty years.

That's all of it, right there. The value of something isn't proportional to the time you can see. It's proportional to the time you can't. To forty years of bad napkins, of lines that didn't work, of learning what to leave out, which is the hardest part of the craft. What the woman thought she was paying for was thirty seconds. What she was paying for was a whole life compressed into thirty seconds. And compressing it like that, making forty years fit into a gesture that looks effortless, that's the real work. Not the drawing. The compression. The drawing is what's left when the compression goes right.

I want to stop here for a moment, because before talking about how unfair it is that it can't be seen, it's worth understanding why making the hard look easy is good in itself. It isn't a trick. It isn't a pose. It's the highest form of mastering something. Anyone can make hard things look hard; there's no merit in that, it's just suffering in public. And plenty of people, without realizing it, make even easy things look hard, piling on unnecessary steps and decorative complexity. But making the hard look easy is something else. It's having walked the road so many times that you've taken out every useless bend. It's knowing what to remove. Mastery isn't adding; it's subtracting until only the essential is left, and you only get there after being wrong enough times to know what actually matters and what was noise.

And there's something more, which is why making the hard look easy isn't just admirable but useful. When you compress the effort, you take friction away from everyone who comes after you. A solution that looks easy is one someone else can use, adopt, and maintain without going through what you went through. A well-made system is one the next guy can run without a thousand-page manual. Making the hard look easy is, deep down, an act of generosity: you swallow the complexity so the rest don't have to. The specialist who explains something in a tangle is handing you their confusion. The one who explains it simply has already done the work of sorting it out for you.

Compressing is also what you do as a generalist, even if you don't call it that while you're doing it. David Epstein wrote a book, *Range*, about why the person who knows a little about many things sometimes solves problems better than the specialist who knows everything about one. I've already written about generalism in another piece in this series, about how problems, not professions, are my unit of measure. But there's an angle I didn't touch then, and it's the one that matters here. The generalist doesn't solve things quickly because they're smarter. They solve quickly because they have samples. Pieces of many fields, patterns seen in one place and recognized in another, analogies the specialist doesn't have on hand because they never left their own court. You take a problem from one world and unlock it with something you understood in another, and from the outside that looks like a magic leap. Like luck. It isn't luck. It's years of collecting samples nobody saw you collect.

And here's the trap of the craft. There are two ways to come off badly. The first is making easy things look hard: complicating the simple on purpose so it looks like you worked a ton. The fifty-slide deck to say something that fit in three. The fancy words to hide that the idea is small. That's puffing up the factory blanket and slapping a "handmade" tag on it, and you can smell it a mile away. The second, the opposite, is quieter and more unfair: making the hard look easy so well that your work gets mistaken for something trivial. Because elegance hides effort; that's its definition. And what's hidden isn't seen. And what isn't seen isn't valued. Not because people are ungrateful, but because they have no way of knowing what it cost someone else.

### II. The violinist in the subway

In 2007 *The Washington Post* ran an experiment that became famous for how brutal the result was. They took Joshua Bell, one of the best violinists in the world, put a Stradivarius worth more than three million dollars in his hands, and stood him up to play in a Washington subway station at rush hour. No poster, no name, no stage. A guy with a violin and the case open on the floor, like any street musician.

He played for forty-five minutes, some of the hardest pieces in the repertoire. More than a thousand people walked by. Almost nobody stopped. A handful tossed in a coin without looking. He collected thirty-two dollars. Three days earlier he'd filled a theater in Boston where people paid a hundred dollars a seat to hear exactly the same thing, on the same violin, with the same hands.

The easy reading of that story is sad: not even the best in the world escapes going unnoticed without a frame that presents him. And it's true. Perceived value needs context. The poster, the price, the seats, the hush of the hall, the collective agreement that what you're about to hear is worth it. Without that frame, pure sound, however perfect, doesn't tell anyone how to stand in front of it.

But there's a better reading, and it's the one I care about. Playing in the subway isn't a failure. If Joshua Bell stood there because he loved playing, because he wanted to feel the violin on an ordinary Tuesday without the solemnity of the theater, then the subway was perfect. The music was the same. The pleasure of playing it, the same or better. The thirty-two dollars only hurt if you expected the subway to pay you like a theater. And that's the key a lot of people miss: the problem isn't playing in the subway. The problem is playing in the subway and expecting to be paid like in Boston.

There are times to play in the subway and times to play in the theater, and the mature thing is knowing which one you're in. When I do something just because I like it, because I want to, because it fills me up, the subway is fine. I don't need the poster. I don't need a thousand people to stop. The reward is having done it. But when what I want is to be valued, to be paid, for the team to stop thinking my blanket came from a big-box store, then I know the subway isn't where you collect that. That calls for the theater. For the frame. For showing the process. Mixing the two up, standing in the subway and getting bitter because it didn't rain money, means you never understood what you wanted in the first place.

There's another story that helps here, about how good things get made. A ceramics teacher split his class into two groups. He told the first that their grade depended on quantity: the more pots they made, the better the grade, regardless of quality. The second, the opposite: one single pot, but perfect. At the end of the semester, the best pots all came from the quantity group. The ones who made dozens of bad pots learned, by getting their hands dirty, things the other group never learned because they spent the semester theorizing about perfection. Quality comes from volume. From accumulated screw-ups. From the ugly pots nobody will ever see, which are the ones that teach you how to make the good one. But since the road gets thrown in the trash and only the final piece remains, people think it came out that way on the first try. They didn't see the hundred before it.

### III. Invisible design

Before oncology, before artificial intelligence, before everything I do today, I studied industrial design. And it left me a principle that explains all this better than anything I learned afterward: good design is invisible.

Dieter Rams put it in a phrase that sounds like a riddle: good design is as little design as possible. A well-designed object doesn't ask for your attention. It doesn't say look at me, notice how well thought-out I am. It just works, so well and so naturally that you stop noticing it. The door handle you grab twenty times a day without a single thought. The chair you're sitting in, which you don't notice is comfortable because it never makes you uncomfortable. The ladle that weighs exactly right, that rests on the rim of the pot without sliding in. Someone thought through every one of those things. Someone spent weeks deciding that angle, that weight, that material. And their reward for getting it perfect is that you will never, not once in your life, think of them while you stir the soup.

That's the most elegant curse there is. The best work is the work that disappears. The work that fits so cleanly into your life that you take it for granted. And taking it for granted is, if you think about it, not counting the cost. It's assuming something was always there, that it just appeared, when in fact someone built it with an effort that, precisely because it was so good, became impossible to see. Perfect effort erases its own footprints.

The most valuable things you build are usually invisible. Not objects. Systems. Processes. Teams that run on their own. Documentation ready so that when you're gone, anyone can take the wheel without the change being noticeable. I've already written in this series about what it feels like to leave and have everything keep running without you. Everything working without you doesn't always mean you're surplus. Sometimes it means the opposite: that you did it so well it became invisible. That you built the door handle so perfectly that nobody notices it. And nobody says thank you to a door handle.

The buffer is invisible by nature. The person who stands in the middle of the chaos and absorbs it so their team can work in peace is invisible, because their success is measured in things that didn't happen, in fires nobody saw because you put them out while they were still sparks. How do you price a fire that never happened? The person who's always ready to fix things delivers their greatest value precisely on the days when nothing happens, because their preparation is the reason nothing happens. But a day without fires looks, from the outside, exactly like a day without work. And that's when the buffer becomes suspicious to anyone who only knows how to measure what they can see.

The invisible isn't valued until it's missing. You only notice the door handle the day it breaks. You only miss the buffer when they leave and the chaos arrives unfiltered. You only appreciate the process when someone tries to replicate your result and discovers, sweating, that it wasn't so easy. That's why the person on my team didn't have a results problem. They had a visibility problem. And visibility problems don't get fixed by working more. They get fixed by letting the work be seen.

### IV. What the theater looks like

Letting the work be seen sounds easy until you try it, because people who make the hard look easy have a kind of modesty about showing effort. We feel that showing the seams takes something away from the blanket. It's the other way around. But since it's counterintuitive, it's worth being concrete about how to do it, because it isn't about going around telling your troubles or demanding applause.

The first thing is to narrate the road, not just hand over the result. When you pass something to someone, instead of dropping the polished final version and leaving it there, briefly say what you went through. "I tried these three ways, and the first two didn't work because of this." That isn't complaining. It's giving the other person the map of the decisions you made, and along the way you show them that the clean result is the survivor of several attempts, not a stroke of luck. People don't need to see the hundred ugly pots, but they do need to know they existed.

The second is to frame before you deliver, not after. Joshua Bell's mistake in the subway wasn't playing; it was not saying a word before he started. A little sign saying who he was would have changed everything. Work is the same: two sentences of context before you show something completely change how it's received. "This looks simple, but the challenge was that it had to work with systems that don't talk to each other." With just that, the person looking knows where to stand.

The third, and this is the one that costs the most, is not to downplay your own work. The number of times people say "no, that was easy," "it was nothing," "I knocked it out real quick" is absurd. Every time you say that, you're erasing the forty years and leaving only the thirty seconds. You don't have to inflate anything. But you don't have to deflate it either. When someone recognizes your work, the least you can do is not rush to explain that it wasn't a big deal. Sometimes visibility is simply quitting the habit of sabotaging it yourself.

And the fourth, the most powerful of all: teach what you know. There's no cleaner way to make the difficulty of something visible than trying to teach it to someone else. The moment someone tries to do what you do and crashes, they understand all at once everything that was behind it. Teaching isn't just generous. It's the most honest way for the other person to grasp the process, because they live it firsthand. The person who watches you solve it thinks it's easy. The person who tries to solve it with you next to them understands that it wasn't.

None of this is blowing your own horn. Blowing your own horn is inflating what isn't there, putting lights on the factory blanket. This is the opposite: giving the other person the information they're missing to value what's in front of them. And, most importantly, it's a tool you use when you want something in return. When you want to be valued, to be paid, for the team to understand. That's when you bring the frame, the theater, the process in plain view. But there are a ton of things you do just because you like them, and those don't need a frame. Those you can play in the subway your whole life without caring that only thirty-two dollars drop into the case.

That's exactly what I told the person on my team. To learn to tell the two apart. That when they were weaving for the love of it, they should weave in peace, without owing anyone an explanation. But that when they wanted the team to stop undervaluing them, they should show their hand: narrate the road, frame before delivering, stop saying everything was easy, and teach what they knew until the others understood what it cost. Not to show off. So people would stop mistaking their handmade blanket for a factory one. Because the work that's really worth something is the work that looks easy. And it's up to you to choose, every time, whether this time you give it away in the subway or charge for it in the theater.
