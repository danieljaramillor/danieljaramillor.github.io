# Don't Make Me Choose

*On generalists, pivots, and the structure that lets you do whatever you want*

When someone asks me what I do, I say I work in tech and I'm independent. It isn't a lie. It's a necessary compression that saves us both the moment where the other person tilts their head, trying to process an answer that doesn't fit any category they know. Because the full answer includes artificial intelligence, oncology, specialty coffee, and a career that runs through crypto, Dubai, and Germany. When my close friends have to explain what I do, they go with "he works in software." The ones who really know me know that doesn't cover even ten percent of it. And every time I try to explain the whole picture, I watch the person get lost, trying to put together a puzzle that isn't the shape they expected.

For a while, that bothered me. It felt like something was broken about not being able to define myself in a single sentence, like not being able to say "I'm X" with the same conviction others say "I'm a lawyer" or "I'm a doctor" was a sign of being scattered, unfocused, not serious about anything.

Today I see it exactly the other way around. Not fitting in a sentence isn't a presentation problem. It's a direct consequence of deciding, a long time ago, that the freedom to do what I want the way I want is worth more than the comfort of a tidy title. And the interesting part is that the confusion I cause, the inability even some of my closest friends have to say what I do, doesn't worry me. It confirms something. If people could explain me easily, it would probably mean I'd shrunk enough to fit into a category that already existed. And fitting into existing categories was never the plan.

That's what this piece is about. What it means to be a generalist in a world that rewards specialization, and how that decision became the most powerful tool I have.

---

## I. The language before the trade

I studied industrial design. I don't practice as an industrial designer in the traditional sense, and by my third semester I was already working in software, so my career took a different road almost from the start. But what that training left me wasn't a trade: it was a language.

Industrial design teaches you to break problems down before you solve them. It teaches you that a well-defined problem doesn't say how or what, only why and for whom. It teaches you that everything is cyclical: define, prototype, test, iterate, define again. That cycle, which the discipline calls design thinking, turned out to be exactly the protocol I've used to walk into every new industry in my life. I don't show up looking for answers. I show up looking for the right questions.

That distinction sounds subtle, but it changes everything. Because when what you bring into new territory isn't a set of answers but a way of interrogating, what the problem is made of becomes secondary. It doesn't matter whether you're picking apart a health system, a decentralized financial market, or the extraction of a competition coffee. The structure of the process is the same: understand before you intervene, define before you propose, iterate before you conclude. More than any technical knowledge, more than any industry experience, what lets me move between fields is knowing how to take a problem apart no matter what material it's made of.

## II. The generalist, seen from the inside

David Epstein gave this way of operating a name in *Range*: the generalist. Someone who walks into a territory, learns it to a level of real competence, takes what they need, and connects it to other territories that seem to have nothing to do with it. Epstein shows that in a complex world, that person has advantages that aren't obvious until you need them: the ability to transfer lessons, to see patterns where a specialist only sees their slice, to solve problems that don't fit in any box.

But *Range* describes the generalist from the research side. What interests me is how it feels to be that person society doesn't know where to put. Not the specialist, whose place is clear. Not the dilettante, who dabbles in everything without going deep. Something in between, harder to name.

Being a generalist means living with a permanent discomfort that, over time, becomes your most valuable tool. It means always being somewhere on the learning curve: sometimes at the peak of unjustified confidence, sometimes in the valley where everything you don't know comes crashing down on you, sometimes on the plateau where competence starts to settle in. And the moment you reach that plateau, something new calls, and you're back at the start. The difference between someone who's crossed that curve many times and someone crossing it for the first time is that the first one recognizes the terrain. They know early confusion isn't incompetence. They know the valley is temporary. And they know that knowledge of one field lights up another in ways you can't anticipate until you're standing in both.

A concrete example: the way patient populations are structured in oncology, the segmentation and classification method that lets you design clinical trials, helped me directly structure a logistics client's purchase orders with an AI model. The logic was the same. What changed was the content, not the architecture. Thinking in structures before content, in questions before answers: that's what a generalist does.

## III. Problems as the unit of measure

There's something it took me a while to understand that completely changed how I see myself: the unit of measure of my life isn't industries. It's problems.

From a distance, my path looks like constant jumping between unrelated fields. Oncology, crypto, artificial intelligence, coffee. The obvious reading is that I'm scattered. But the sequence has a different logic, because what connects one stage to the next isn't the sector but the kind of problem I was solving. And every problem I solved taught me something about solving the next one, whatever industry it lived in.

It's as if problems share a grammar. A segmentation problem is a segmentation problem, whether it's called patient populations or purchase categories. A scale problem is a scale problem, whether it's in a tech startup or a clinical operation. A problem of communication between worlds that speak different languages is the same problem everywhere, and the skill of translating between those worlds is the same skill no matter which worlds they are.

Once I started seeing it that way, the idea of "pivoting" lost the dramatic weight we usually give it. A pivot isn't a change of identity. It isn't a reboot. It's just picking the next problem. And if the way you approach problems is solid, if your columns are the process and not the content, then the next problem can be in any industry in the world and you'll have the tools to face it. Not the answers. The tools. Which is far more valuable, because answers expire and tools don't.

This is something I think professional culture gets fundamentally wrong. We're taught to think in careers, in straight-line trajectories, in the vertical accumulation of experience inside a single field. "How many years of experience do you have in X?" is the question that decides whether you deserve a seat at the table. That logic works in a predictable world, where the field you walked into at twenty-five will still exist in the same form when you're fifty. But that world is gone. Industries merge, transform, disappear. Problems, on the other hand, persist. They change shape and context, but their deep structure repeats. And the person who learned to read those structures is the one who can move without breaking when the world rearranges itself.

The funny thing is that this way of thinking has a consequence nobody warns you about: the more problems you solve, the better you get at solving problems. Not just faster or more efficient, though that happens too. Better in the sense that you start recognizing patterns between problems that have nothing to do with each other. That a customer-retention problem and a cancer-treatment-adherence problem share the same structure of incentives and friction. That a startup's cash-flow problem and a hospital's resource-allocation problem are variations on the same score. The industry changes. The grammar of the problem repeats.

And that creates a kind of compounding effect. Each new problem doesn't start from zero; it starts from the inventory of patterns you already recognize. What takes you months to understand early in your career later takes weeks, then days. Not because you're smarter, but because your library of structures is bigger. And that library has no industry label. It's yours. It travels with you. It works wherever you put it.

In the second piece I wrote about the difference between collecting information and really understanding things. This is an extension of that idea. I'm not interested in collecting industries the way some people collect stamps. I'm interested in understanding the problems each industry poses, solving them with the tools I already have, picking up the new tools the process hands me, and moving on. The accumulation I care about isn't titles or sector experience. It's the capacity to solve.

## IV. The cost nobody tells you about

I don't want to romanticize it. Being a generalist has a cost the specialist doesn't pay, and it would be dishonest to talk about the advantages without talking about the bill.

The first cost is the recurring feeling of not being enough in any field. Of always being a step behind the person who's spent ten years in the discipline. Of knowing enough to see what's missing, but not enough to fix it without help. That feeling is real, and it doesn't go away with experience. What changes is your relationship with it. At first it paralyzes you. Over time you recognize it as a companion that keeps you honest. It makes you listen before you speak. It makes you ask questions the experts stopped asking long ago. And sometimes, only sometimes, those questions from the newest person at the table are exactly the ones that unlock something the veterans couldn't see because they were too far inside.

The second is intellectual loneliness. My day-to-day is a constant fork: I go from an AI meeting to an oncology meeting in the same block of time, back to back, switching vocabulary, conceptual frame, and the kind of person across the table. People in both worlds know I exist in the other one, but almost nobody lives in both at once. That means there are connections I see, transfers I make, patterns I recognize, that I can't easily share with anyone because they need context from two worlds to make sense. It's like being bilingual somewhere every person speaks only one of your two languages. You can talk to everyone, but there are things you can only say in the language the other person doesn't speak.

And the third, maybe the most insidious, is how easily a single industry can kidnap you. It isn't that you choose to wall yourself in. It's that the environment closes in around you. The conversations narrow. Your social circle lines up. Your professional identity compresses into a single word. And one day you realize that pivoting isn't a free choice anymore; it's a demolition. Because you put in so many finishes, so much facade, so much definition, that changing direction would mean tearing down everything you built. What saved me from getting trapped was that my columns were never an industry. My columns were always the process, the way of breaking problems down. And that travels with you anywhere. The industry is the facade. The process is the structure. And when the facade falls, which it will, because they all fall eventually, what matters is that the structure is still standing.

In *Shantaram*, Gregory David Roberts writes from a place I recognize: that of a man who has lived too many lives for a single label to describe him. Fugitive, improvised doctor in the slums of Bombay, soldier, forger, prisoner. What makes that novel more than an adventure is that Roberts never tries to reconcile those versions of himself into a clean narrative. He lets them coexist. He lets them contradict each other. And in that coexistence he finds something that looks a lot like the truth: that a life really lived doesn't fit in an executive summary. What connects Roberts's lives isn't a theme. It's a way of being in the world: intensely, unfiltered, completely willing to get into whatever comes and pay the consequences. That doesn't fit in a sentence either. And that's exactly the point.

## V. Dying to pivot

Tolkien understood something about transformation that most people would rather not look at head-on. Gandalf the Grey dies so Gandalf the White can be born. Aragorn is a ranger before he's a king. The Frodo who leaves the Shire is not the hobbit who comes back to it. None of them can be defined in a single sentence, because each sentence would leave out a version of them that was necessary to reach the next. And that, in Middle-earth as in real life, isn't being scattered. It's depth.

Someone I met recently, with whom conversation has the quality good pairings have (it's not that one needs the other, it's that together something appears that didn't exist apart), introduced me to an interview with Mario Mendoza that connects directly to this: that you shouldn't be afraid of dying. He isn't talking about physical death. He's talking about letting the versions of yourself that have done their job die. About not clinging to who you were out of fear of not knowing who you'll be.

That idea seems central to understanding what a generalist does every time they pivot. It isn't just changing industries. It's letting a version of you die that was alive and working, a version that had a name, a reputation, a clear place in the world, to make room for something that doesn't have a shape yet. That takes a very specific kind of confidence: not the confidence of knowing what comes next, but the confidence of knowing your columns can take the move. That who you really are doesn't depend on the facade you happen to be wearing.

Some people are terrified of that transition. They need to know exactly what's next before they let go of what they have. They need a guarantee that the next floor will be better than the last. And I get it. But that need for certainty is what produces finished buildings, perfect finishes that can't move, lives that look flawless on the outside and have not one inch of room to grow on the inside.

What I've learned, through the successive deaths of versions of myself, is that the signal to pivot doesn't come from analysis. It comes from something more basic. It's a feeling. An instinct that switches on when something new pushes the right buttons, when a problem calls you with an urgency you can't explain rationally but recognize because you've felt it before. It isn't a career plan. It's an internal compass that runs on enthusiasm, not strategy. And the only way to keep that compass calibrated is to not let any industry, any identity, any finish cover it up.

That compass has no instruction manual. I can't say exactly what sets it off or why certain problems pull at me harder than others. What I can say is that the feeling is unmistakable. It's the same one I had when I got into oncology and knew there was something there I needed to understand. The same one I had when crypto showed me a financial system that could be taken apart and put back together. The same one I feel now with artificial intelligence, where every week there's a new problem that didn't exist the week before. It's an enthusiasm you can't manufacture or force. It shows up or it doesn't. And when it shows up, what you need isn't to analyze it but to follow it, trusting that the structure you carry is solid enough to hold you while you find out where it's taking you.

In the second piece I wrote about snobbery as curiosity followed all the way through. About the difference between the snob who wants to be seen understanding and the one who genuinely wants to understand why things are the way they are. The same logic applies here. I don't chase industries because they look good on a profile. I chase them because each one puts in front of me a set of problems I get obsessed with, and that obsession is the only compass I've ever needed. I want to understand why things work the way they do, and when I stop wanting to understand, it's time to go. Not out of infidelity but out of honesty. Because staying somewhere I've stopped learning would be the real scattering, the worst kind: scattering my attention on something that no longer deserves it.

## VI. Paying attention as a way of life

After the second piece in this series, I started getting messages I didn't expect. Not quick comments, but long reflections from people who had taken the time to read and think before replying. One of them said something that stayed with me: "When you become friends with someone like you, you stop being shallow and actually start paying attention to things. It's like you're really paying attention to life instead of running on autopilot."

I read it several times. Not out of vanity, but because it named something I hadn't managed to put into words: that being a generalist, deep down, is nothing more than paying attention to more things than you're supposed to. Refusing to accept that your curiosity has to fit in a single field. Insisting that the world is more interesting when you look at it from several angles at once. And that insistence, which some people read as being scattered or unable to focus, is actually the opposite: a kind of attention so wide it makes people who chose to look in one direction uncomfortable.

My method for entering new territory is always the same: I assume nobody will be able to teach me everything I need to know. Not out of arrogance, but because the way I need to understand something rarely matches the way other people explain it. So I start with two questions: what can I teach myself, and where is that knowledge going to come from? Then I jump. With the benefit of the doubt. With the benefit of trying. With the absolute certainty that I'm going to screw up a lot, and with the peace of knowing those screw-ups are the most honest building material there is.

I'm going to compete in the national brewers championship. I've been at coffee for years, but competing is something else. It's going back to the start of the curve, knowing the valley is waiting on the other side, and going in anyway. Because at this point, after so many cycles, what I enjoy most isn't reaching the other side. It's the crossing. The process itself of going from knowing nothing to knowing something, from screwing up to screwing up less, from being the new guy to being someone who can sit at the table without his voice shaking. That process is, for me, the closest thing I know to being completely alive.

I don't want this to sound like a motivational speech or an invitation for everyone to go change careers tomorrow. Everyone has their own way of being in the world, and deep specialization is just as valid as generalism. Some problems can only be solved with twenty years of total immersion in a single field, and the people who do that deserve all the respect in the world. What I do believe, with a conviction that has hardened with every problem solved and every industry crossed, is that the obsession with finishing yourself, with reaching a point where everything is defined and solved and boxed up, is one of the most elegant traps there is. Because it dresses up as maturity when it's really fear. Fear of not knowing. Fear of being the new guy. Fear of screwing up. Fear that people won't be able to explain who you are at a dinner party.

Gary Hustwit documents that same tension in *Rams*. Dieter Rams, the man who defined modern industrial design, says at the end of his career that design is never finished. That every object is an iteration, not a destination. When I first saw that documentary, I felt I was watching the distilled version of what my career had taught me without my being able to name it: that the process matters more than the result, that today's best solution is tomorrow's starting point, and that whoever thinks they've finished designing something has stopped paying attention to it. If the man who shaped almost everything we touch accepts that his work was never complete, why do we insist our lives should be?

---

## VII. Bare-brick gratitude

There's a gratitude that only shows up when you stop asking the world for permission to be who you are. It isn't the gratitude of someone receiving a gift. It's the gratitude of someone who built something with their own hands, with materials nobody else would have picked, following a blueprint nobody else would have approved, and who looks at what's in front of them and feels, with a clarity that needs no outside validation, that they're exactly where they want to be.

I'm grateful to every industry that let me in without asking for credentials. To every problem that made me feel stupid for weeks before it started to give. To every person who looked at me like "this guy has no idea what he's talking about" and who, without knowing it, was handing me the best motivation there is. I'm grateful for the screw-ups, and there were many, because each one left me a tool no degree would have given me. And above all, I'm grateful I didn't listen to the voice that told me so many times to pick one thing and sit still.

I didn't sit still. I'm not going to sit still. And that isn't a challenge or a promise. It's simply the most accurate description I have of how I work. I'm headstrong, as people have told me my whole life, and it turns out my head has taken me places I'd never have found if I'd waited for someone to hand me a map.

I still don't fit in a sentence. And at this point, honestly, I wouldn't want to even if I could.
