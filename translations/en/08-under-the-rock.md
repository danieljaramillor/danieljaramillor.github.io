# Under the Rock

*On attention that runs short, the shells you have to shed, and why it's okay not to manage everything*

It's one in the morning and I'm sitting in the room I grew up in. The same room that has had three different desks, a coffee bar, a sofa, more screens than NASA, a giant shelf where I tried to fit all my gadgets and hobbies and passions at once. Now it has, for the first time in its existence, a bed big enough for two. It has no desk. It has the remains of a coffee bar that now only travels in my suitcase. It has a giant shelf, mostly empty. This room has changed almost as many times as I have, and at this point it's more me than I am it.

I open my phone and see everything at once. The emails I haven't answered. The calendar with three weeks of meetings stacked up. The to-dos that multiply on their own. The bills. The half-built projects. The unread messages. And in among all of it, as if hiding in the avalanche, the names. Friends I haven't talked to in weeks. Some in months. People I really love, not in the light way you love people on social media but the real way, the kind with history, the kind with weight. I see my entire life on a six-inch screen. And as I look at it, I feel something I need to say out loud, because if I don't, it stays clenched inside.

I'm full. And I can't keep up.

I've been a bad friend. Not for lack of love. For an excess of everything else. This essay is my way of facing that. Of putting it into words. Of admitting that the guy who remembers everything people say and buys gifts in airports because he thought of someone who doesn't even know he thought of them, that same guy sometimes goes weeks without sending a message. And that this isn't okay. But also, and this is the important part, that it's okay to admit it isn't okay.

---

### I. Four thousand weeks

Oliver Burkeman wrote a book called *Four Thousand Weeks*. That's roughly the time you get if you live to eighty. When I read it, it did the thing books that actually change something do: it didn't teach me anything I didn't know. It made me look straight at something I'd rather not see. Because it's one thing to know time is finite and another to sit down and do the math and get four thousand weeks and have that be it. Your whole life. Everything you'll live, think, feel, build, screw up, fix, and share. Four thousand weeks. And you've already spent a pile of them.

Four thousand weeks. It doesn't sound like much when you put it in numbers. And into those four thousand weeks you have to fit everything: work, projects, relationships, health, rest, obsessions, mistakes, learning, one a.m. coffees, four-thousand-word essays in the small hours. Everything. And what Burkeman argues is that the trap isn't that time is short. The trap is that we live as if it were infinite. That we make plans as if we had time for everything. And that the anxiety you feel when you can't keep up isn't a sign you're doing something wrong. It's a sign you're finite and pretending you're not.

That idea hit me because that's how I live. The Daniel who leads a team. The Daniel who writes essays at dawn. The Daniel who researches markets. The Daniel who tries to sleep well, make coffee slowly, and have a life that isn't just a to-do list dressed up as purpose. All those Daniels share the same brain and the same twenty-four hours. And what's left at the end of the day sometimes isn't enough to sit down and talk to a friend properly. Not a dutiful "what's up?" answered with "good, you?" where the conversation dies. Real presence. The kind where you listen without your head planning the week's sprint while the other person tells you something that matters to them.

And it isn't only people. It's projects too. Building your own reality, as I've talked about in this series, sounds inspiring until you're inside it and realize the reality you built has more open fronts than one person can tend. Every project is a fig you picked off the branch. Every idea that became something real is a responsibility that adds up. And at some point the sum exceeds capacity. Not because you're mediocre. Because you're finite. And finitude, when you're used to operating as if it didn't exist, feels like betrayal. As if your own brain were failing you. But it isn't failing you. It's telling you the truth.

Sometimes a project needs you to let it go, not because it's bad but because it isn't the time. Sometimes an idea that promised everything has to go in the freezer, like the pho broth, and wait until there are hands to prepare it properly. And telling the difference between "this doesn't work" and "this works but I can't right now" is one of the hardest calls I make. Because letting go doesn't feel like efficiency. It feels like defeat. Even when it isn't. And you have to learn to live with that feeling without letting it freeze you.

But I want to be clear here, because this essay isn't a complaint: it's okay. It's okay to have hard weeks. It's okay not to keep up. It's okay to feel overwhelmed on a Tuesday at six p.m. with no energy for anything except making a coffee and staring at the ceiling. It's okay to have a phone full of unread messages and not know where to start. It's okay to open the calendar on a Sunday night and feel like next week is already beating you before it starts. Life happens. Work happens. The weeks stack up like dirty dishes and sometimes there's no energy left even to wash them. And the pressure to pretend you can handle it all, that everything is always fine, that your capacity has no ceiling, that pressure is worse than the overload itself. Because the overload is real and can be managed. The lie that it doesn't exist is what breaks you. What destroys you isn't having too much on your plate. It's pretending you don't.

### II. The lobster

Rabbi Abraham Twerski told a story about lobsters that explains discomfort. The lobster is a soft animal inside a rigid shell. That shell doesn't expand. So as the lobster grows inside, it starts to feel pressure, discomfort, stress. And what it does is find a rock, hide under it, cast off the old shell, and wait, vulnerable, for a new one to grow. Then it comes out. Lives again. Until the new one gets tight too. And back under the rock it goes. Its whole life.

If lobsters had doctors, they'd never grow. Because as soon as they felt the discomfort, they'd go to the doctor, get a prescription for painkillers, feel fine, and never shed the shell. The signal disappears. And without the signal, there's no growth.

I've been feeling the shell get tight for a while now. Not because of work, not money, not any of the problems people assume when you're building your own thing. It's something quieter. The feeling that I'm failing at something that has no dashboard, no metric, no KPI. Relationships. Presence. The attention I owe the people I love and haven't been giving them. And at the same time, the projects that need more of me than I'm giving them. The double debt. The lobster getting squeezed from every side at once.

Sylvia Plath, in *The Bell Jar*, described a fig tree with a fig on every branch, each one a possible life. And the protagonist sitting at the foot of the tree, paralyzed, watching the figs wrinkle and fall because she can't choose. I don't identify with her. I don't sit there staring at the figs. I grab one. If it wasn't the right one, I drop it and grab another. That's how I work. That's how I've always worked. My problem is the opposite of Plath's: I grab too many figs at once and don't have enough hands to hold them all. And the ones that fall aren't always the ones you'd expect. The bad projects don't fall. Those I drop fairly easily, as I told in the last essay with the 3D crystals and the pocket aces. The ones that fall are the ones that need a kind of attention you can't automate, fit into a two-week sprint, or solve with a quick text. Relationships. Long conversations. Friends who need you to be there, not to fix something for them. Those figs slip out while you're holding the others. And by the time you notice they've fallen, weeks have passed. Sometimes months. And the fallen fig doesn't rot like in Plath's novel. It stays there on the ground, waiting for you to pick it up. But every day it gets a little harder to bend down for it, because bending down means letting go of what's in your hands, and what's in your hands feels urgent even when what's on the ground matters more.

Recently a friend I made on Clubhouse, back when hopping into voice rooms to talk with strangers in the middle of the pandemic was a normal pastime and the only kind of interaction possible, wrote to me. I have a lot of friends I met on Clubhouse. Also a lot I met through my VR headset. Both during the pandemic, both from those moments when people looked for human connection wherever they could find it. Anyway, she wrote and said she liked my essays, how present, how reflective, how very everything. But that it had been a WHILE since we'd talked. What was up with that? That she needed attention. That it wasn't fair that half the internet knew what I was thinking while she, who actually knows me, had gone months without knowing how I was.

And she was completely right.

I told her yes, that I wanted to do everything and connect with everyone but that I remembered the people right next to me more. That I was being a bad friend. That it wasn't intentional, but the result was the same. That I'd do better. And as I said it, I knew it was true, and that it was also the same promise I make myself every time I realize I've gone weeks without asking how people are. The intention is always there. The execution is what fails. And between intention and execution, there's room for a whole lifetime of carelessness dressed up as good intentions.

That conversation is exactly what Twerski describes. The shell getting tight. The signal you can't ignore. My friend wasn't scolding me. She was giving me the feedback I didn't ask for, the kind I wrote in another essay is the most valuable there is. And she was giving it with love, with the trust of someone who knows she can tell you how things are because the relationship can take it. That's a privilege. And treating it as one, taking it without getting defensive, without making excuses, without saying "I've just been really busy," is part of what it means to grow.

### III. The rocks

My rock varies. Sometimes my rock is my friends, my family. People who know me well enough to know that when I'm quiet, it isn't because I don't care. Sometimes it's being alone. Shutting everything down. Disappearing from the world for a while and letting the discomfort do its work without trying to fix it right away. And sometimes my rock is making coffee.

Making coffee, for me, is a meditative act. I say that without irony. The process of grinding, heating the water to the exact temperature, preparing a pour-over with the patience specialty coffee demands, is one of the few moments in the day when my head stays on a single thing. No multitasking. No scrolling. No versions of me competing for attention. When I'm under a lot of stress, I make a lot of coffee. I keep decaf and low-caffeine beans; I'm not a walking tremor. But the act of making it, of giving ten minutes to something that isn't productive or urgent or necessary, is my way of getting under the rock. Of pausing. Of letting the shell do its thing without trying to rush it. Of reminding myself that not everything has to be efficient to have value. That sometimes the most useful thing you can do is something completely useless.

Last Thursday I hosted a game night at my place. I pulled wine from the cellar, made five-spice wings with chili oil and some truffle fries. I invited people. The idea was to play something, mess around, hang out. Nobody played anything. At some point in the night the conversation went somewhere else and nobody stopped it. We started talking about our pain. About small traumas we carry. About things that aren't so small, that steal our peace and that we haven't managed to resolve yet. Helped along by the wine, helped along by the trust of knowing nobody at that table would judge, five people sitting around eating wings and telling each other who we are, unfiltered. Without the "good, you?" mask. Without the facade that everything's perfect.

I was very present that night. And I knew it while it was happening. I didn't think about work. I didn't think about essays. I didn't think about the pipeline or the metrics or anything with a screen. I was there. With those five people. Building and unbuilding ourselves together. That was the rock working. That was dropping every fig but one and holding it with both hands. And it was eleven-thirty at night before we noticed the time. Nobody wanted to leave. Nobody wanted to break what had formed.

That confirmed something for me: the problem isn't that I don't know how to be present. When I'm there, I'm really there. When I give attention, I give all of it. The problem is frequency. The gap between one night like that and the next fills up with silence and good intentions that never happen. It fills up with "I'll call them next week," which becomes "next month, for sure," which becomes silence. And silence, when it comes from someone who loves you, doesn't sound like silence. It sounds like abandonment. Even when it isn't.

Something similar happens with projects. When I sit down to work on something I care about, the intensity is total. No half measures. But I have more things I care about than fit in a week. And every yes I give a project is presence I take from another. Every hour I give a new front is an hour I don't give a person. And I've spent a good while adding without looking at what I was subtracting.

Ethan Hawke said: *"The sun doesn't care whether the grass appreciates its rays, right? It just keeps on shining."* I like that idea. But sometimes the sun clouds over. Not because it stops wanting to shine. Because energy isn't infinite. And admitting that isn't weakness. It's the most honest thing I can say from this room at one-thirty in the morning.

### IV. What goes unsaid

Right before I published the last essay, Camilo Rojas, an artist from Cali, painted a canvas that says: "Either you live your fears or you live your dreams." It felt like the most beautiful coincidence. Two people from the same city, by completely different roads, bringing out what they carry inside at the same moment without knowing the other was doing it. Cali has these things. Something about this city pushes you to bring out what's inside. Maybe it's the heat. Maybe it's the music. Maybe it's that people here don't hold back on being kind, and it rubs off on you.

To live your dreams, you have to get past your fears. But not all at once. Like the lobster: one shell at a time. One fear at a time. And between one shell and the next there's a moment when you're completely exposed, and that's not only okay. It's necessary. Because if you're never exposed, you never grow. And if you never grow, what you have isn't safety. It's stagnation with air conditioning.

After "Dancing Without Choreography," people wrote to me. People who've spent months debating whether to quit. People afraid to start something of their own. People who'd closed chapters. People who told me they read the essay three times before making a decision they'd been putting off. Every message confirmed something: the discomfort I feel isn't only mine. What changes is the shell. What changes is the rock. But the mechanics are the same for everyone.

What I do with this series is not hide the discomfort. It's write it down. And what I get back is people telling me: me too. That's what goes unsaid. Not on social media, where everyone's fine. Not at work, where showing cracks is risky. Not among founders, where admitting you can't handle everything feels like admitting you shouldn't be where you are. Not among friends, where saying "I haven't talked to you in weeks because I don't have time" sounds like an excuse even when it's the truest thing you could say in that moment. Real discomfort stays inside because there's nowhere to let it out without it sounding like complaining. I try to make these essays that place. At least for me. And apparently for more people than I imagined.

And I want to say something to those people, the ones who write to me, the ones who read and don't write, the ones under their own rock right now: it's okay. It's okay to have weeks when you can't keep up. It's okay to feel the shell getting tight. It's okay to have projects in the freezer and friends in the to-do pile and a life that sometimes feels like juggling more balls than you have hands. What's not okay is pretending you can handle it all. What's not okay is the painkiller. What's not okay is silencing the signal. Because the signal is what tells you you're growing. And growing hurts. It always has. What matters is what you do with the pain: hide it or use it.

What I've learned through broken shells is that when everything's tight, you go back to bare brick. To the principles. To knowing who you are when the noise gets loud. Not who you want to be. Who you really are, cracks and all. The self-doubt you feel when you can't keep up, that dissatisfaction with yourself, is normal. It's the lobster feeling the pressure. But underneath that pressure are all the shells you've already shed, all the times you grew, all the evidence that you can come out bigger than before. I always tell myself the same thing when things get hard: *fear is the mind-killer*, as Frank Herbert says in *Dune*. And if you feel something squeezing, it's because what you're doing has weight. What doesn't matter doesn't squeeze. What doesn't matter doesn't keep you up at two in the morning in the room you grew up in.

### V. Coming out from under the rock

Hawke also said, that same night: *"The one who's in love is always winning. It doesn't matter if you get your heart broken. You're living."* And that applies to everything you do for real. Writing essays that leave you exposed. Opening a voice room on Clubhouse during a pandemic to talk with strangers. Putting on a VR headset to connect with people on the other side of the world because it was the only way not to feel alone. Inviting people over, pulling wine from the cellar, making wings and truffle fries, and ending up talking about trauma at one-thirty in the morning. Starting a project knowing it might not work. Letting go of a project that works because it isn't the time. Sending a message you should have sent weeks ago and saying "hey, sorry for being a bad friend."

Each of those things is a way of coming out from under the rock. Of showing the new shell even while it's still soft. Of telling the world "here I am, still growing, and you're going to watch me grow in public because that's how I work." Life is finite, the shell gets tight, the figs fall, and the sun sometimes clouds over. But the alternative is not growing, not grabbing any fig, not shining, not feeling. And that alternative, the painkiller, the comfort that silences the signal, is the only one that's actually frightening.

The "I'll take care of it" philosophy I built in this series is still intact. What changed is what it includes. Because precisely because I take care of things, I have to take care of this too. Of the attention I owe the people at the center of my onion. Of the projects that need me to tell them "not now" so I can say "yes, now" to the people who need it. Of not letting the construction of my reality eat the relationships that give that reality its meaning. Because a life built with your own hands but with no one to share it isn't independence. It's loneliness with nice finishes. And I don't want nice finishes on an empty building. I want bare brick with people inside. I want the room I grew up in, which has changed as many times as I have, to keep filling up with nights like that Thursday. With wings and wine and conversations nobody planned. With real presence, even if it's intermittent. Even if it sometimes fails. Even if the sun sometimes clouds over and the weeks fill with silence.

And if you're reading this, my friends, and you feel like I've been half ignoring you: hit me up. Don't wait for me to get organized. Let me know. Scold me. Tell me "hey, you're being a bad friend." Because sometimes I'm so deep in my own stuff I don't notice. And that reminder is exactly what I need.

It's two in the morning. The coffee's gone cold. The rock is temporary. What grows under it isn't.
