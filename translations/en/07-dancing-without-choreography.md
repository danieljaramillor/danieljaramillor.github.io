# Dancing Without Choreography

*On choosing yourself, what it costs, and why the goal isn't to arrive but to be here*

I heard a Jim Carrey commencement speech while YouTube autoplay was running as background noise. That's how I find a lot of the things that end up staying with me forever: without looking, letting the algorithm do its thing while I do mine. I must have been cooking, or cleaning, or doing something that didn't need all my attention, and suddenly a line stopped me cold.

Carrey's father wanted to be a comedian. He had the talent, he had the gift. But he didn't believe that was possible for someone like him. He did what most people do: he chose the safe option. He became an accountant. A stable job, a predictable paycheck, a life designed not to fall apart. And when Jim was twelve, he got fired. The safe job turned out not to be so safe. And the lesson Jim took from that is one of the most honest things I've ever heard about risk: *"You can fail at what you don't want, so you might as well take a chance on doing what you love."*

That line haunts me because it describes exactly the bet I make with my life. Not made. Make. Every day. Every company, every project, every decision to take the road that isn't in the manual is a version of the same bet: if I'm going to lose, I'd rather lose with something that's mine. In this series I've talked about building your own reality, about expecting nothing from anyone, about giving opinions with the weight of experience behind them. But I've never talked about what it feels like in the body when you make those decisions. About the anxiety. About the lies you tell yourself so you can keep moving. About what happens when you look inward and what you find isn't as heroic as what you tell the outside world.

---

### I. The anxiety of being your own

Carl Sagan said that if you wish to make an apple pie from scratch, you must first invent the universe. I love that line because it's absurd and true at the same time. Nothing exists in isolation. Everything you build depends on layers that came before: experiences, mistakes, people, contexts you didn't choose. The earlier essays in this series are the record of how that universe was built. The bare brick, the archiving, the opinions that cost something, attention as a way of loving. Those are the layers. This essay is about what happens when you decide to take the step.

And the first thing that happens is anxiety.

Not the clinical kind, or the kind that paralyzes. Another kind. One that isn't in the manuals but that anyone who's chosen their own path knows. The anxiety of knowing your decisions are yours. That when you choose to be your own person, when you take control of the variables that on any other path would be spread across systems, bosses, structures that absorb the risk for you, that spreading disappears. Everything is yours. The wins, the failures, the ambiguous decisions where there's no right answer and you have to move anyway. Your time, your money, your reputation, your word. All of it on the table.

The first time I started a company, my parents were very critical. Not out of malice. Out of fear. They told me I should protect myself, not risk so much. And I understood them, because what they saw was their son betting his time, money, and reputation on something with no guarantee of working. What they couldn't see from where they stood was that for me the risk wasn't trying. The risk was not doing it and living with the doubt. When the company started showing a few signs of success, the tune changed. Not because they were inconsistent. Because the evidence changed. And that taught me something I still apply today: people will criticize your decision until it works. Your job isn't to convince them in advance. It's to disprove their hypotheses with facts.

My parents, it has to be said, always gave me feedback I never asked for. They were critical when they had to be. But they were also there every time I screwed up. Every time something didn't work out. Every time I needed support without asking. Both things are true at once: they questioned me and they held me up. And that combination is the most honest way to love someone who's choosing a path you don't fully understand.

Choosing yourself produces an adrenaline that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't felt it. It's like an extreme sport with no way to get off. Your life is in your hands more than in any other scenario. At first it's uncomfortable. Then it becomes addictive. You start looking for the feeling. Needing it. And that addiction comes with lies you tell yourself so you can keep going. The most common one, in my case: "I can handle this alone." Sometimes it's true. Often it isn't. But I want to try anyway. Because that's where I learn, where I stretch, where I find out whether the limit I thought I had was real or made up.

---

### II. The voices that aren't yours

One day, not long ago, someone who loves me told me, with the best intentions in the world, that what I was doing was too risky. That I should look for something more stable. That with what I know I could land a comfortable position at any company and stop living on a tightrope. And while she talked, I didn't hear concern for me. I heard her own fear speaking through advice. She wasn't seeing me. She was seeing the version of herself that would have been afraid to do what I do. And she was asking me to be afraid too.

That happens more than people think. Many of the expectations put on you have nothing to do with you. They have to do with the person putting them there. With what they didn't do. With what scared them. With what they set aside. That doesn't make them bad people. It makes them human. But projection, when it isn't recognized as projection, becomes a cage.

You see it a lot between parents and children. Parents who gave something up to raise their kids and who, consciously or not, instill ideals in them that don't belong to them. Sometimes through guilt, sometimes through a sense of debt: you have to do X because I gave up X to have you. Don't make the same mistakes I did. And they cover their children's eyes so they'll only fly in the direction they themselves couldn't. That isn't cruelty. It's miscalibrated love. But the result is the same: a person who grows up believing they owe someone something for existing.

You don't owe anyone anything. Not your father, not your partner, not any friend. You never have to do or not do something you truly want because someone told you they couldn't. As long as you understand where it's coming from. Because understanding isn't dismissing. It's separating what helps from what weighs you down. It's saying: I hear you, I understand your fear, and I'm going to do what I need to do anyway.

But to be fair: not everything that comes from outside is projection. Some people give you clean, honest feedback, based on what they genuinely believe your situation is. People who take the trouble to understand where you are before they weigh in. That feedback is worth something. What changes everything is where they're speaking from: one person speaks from their fear, the other from your context. You learn to tell them apart by getting it wrong. By listening to the wrong person. By archiving the lesson.

And there's something else I learned over time: there are two kinds of people who come to you for help when problems hit. People who really listen, who take what you tell them, process it, and move. And people who just need you to listen and validate what they feel. They're not looking for a solution. They're looking for company in the hole. That wears you down. And you have to be careful how much energy you give each kind, because emotional generosity without limits empties you out.

Carrey said it with a clarity that hurts: *"So many of us choose our path out of fear disguised as practicality."* And fear disguised as practicality looks exactly like a good decision. Until it isn't.

### III. The impostor who builds

Impostor syndrome isn't just the voice that says "I'm not enough." It's something worse. It's the pile-up of all those outside voices, internalized until you can't tell which are yours and which were put on you. Your father's voice telling you not to take risks, turned into your own voice telling you that you don't deserve to be where you are. Someone else's standard measuring work that's yours. The impostor doesn't come from nowhere. It's built from other people's expectations, stacked up without anyone asking permission.

The answer, at least mine, is to go back to bare brick. To the principles I've built over this series. To knowing who I am before asking whether I'm enough. Because "am I enough?" has no answer if you don't know enough for what and according to whom. And if the "according to whom" turns out to be a voice that isn't yours, the question falls apart on its own.

The Pareto principle says eighty percent of results come from twenty percent of the effort. I've operated that way since high school. If I could get good results with twenty percent of the input, my time went to something I really enjoyed, or to doing more things: more eighty-percents. And that isn't being mediocre. It's knowing where the twenty percent that matters is and going all in there. *Done is better than perfect*, not because perfection doesn't matter, but because perfection that never ships doesn't exist. I'd rather have eighty percent out in the world than a hundred percent in my head.

And eventually something comes along that consumes you. A project, an obsession that grabs you at night and won't let go. Something you want to do at a hundred percent. And you do. But even there, even in that total obsession, you break it into pieces. And some of them get eighty. Because efficiency doesn't fight with passion. It complements it. It lets you give a hundred to what really deserves it because you didn't burn yourself out on things that worked perfectly well with less.

Anthony Bourdain said bad food is made without pride, by people who have no pride and no love. That it's food that shows fear and a lack of confidence in people's ability to make their own decisions. That applies to everything. Work done out of fear, designed not to offend, to cover every base without committing to any, shows. It has a particular taste. The taste of not having placed a bet. And people can tell, even if they can't name it.

Miyamoto Musashi, the samurai who wrote *The Book of Five Rings* before he died, left a line I repeat like a mantra: *"You can only fight the way you practice."* If your practice is solid, your improvisation will be solid. If your base is firm, the moment of pressure doesn't destroy you. It reveals you.

That connects to something I care a lot about, something that comes from my city: salsa. Improvising in salsa isn't bad. If you know the turns, if you know the steps, if you can read your partner, improvisation isn't chaos. It's freedom inside a structure. You dance without choreography, but you know the turns. That's mastery. But improvising without knowing, without having built the base, without having trained your ear or your feet, and believing you'll dance on your first try at the level of someone who's spent years on the floor: that's delusion. Sagan tells you without telling you: to make the pie, first invent the universe.

I've staked my reputation and my word on things that didn't work. And what I'm left with from those falls isn't shame. It's calibration. Every mistake recalibrates the instrument. Every screw-up sharpens the filter. And paradoxically, every time I'm wrong I become more cautious and more daring at the same time. More cautious because I know what being wrong costs. More daring because I know I survived last time and can survive the next.

Bourdain knew it too: *"I always entertain the notion that I'm wrong, or that I'll have to revise my opinion. Most of the time that feels good; sometimes it really hurts and is embarrassing."* That's the opposite of the impostor. The impostor tells you that you don't deserve to be where you are. Honesty says: I'm here, I might be wrong, and I'm going to keep going anyway.

### IV. Knowing when to let go

In *The Fellowship of the Ring*, when Frodo tells Gandalf he's late, Gandalf answers: *"A wizard is never late, Frodo Baggins. Nor is he early. He arrives precisely when he means to."* I love that line. I love *The Lord of the Rings* with the intensity of someone who doesn't apologize for being a nerd. And this line I actually believe. I believe nothing arrives when you want it to, but it doesn't arrive when you don't, either. Opportunities don't ask permission. They just exist. And they're only useful to the person ready to see them.

But knowing when to let go is as important as knowing when to act.

In poker there's a concept every serious player knows: sometimes you have to fold pocket aces. The best possible hand before the flop. But if the community cards have changed the game, if the circumstances are telling you your perfect hand isn't perfect anymore, clinging to it for what it promised is the most elegant way to lose everything. The hardest part isn't losing what you invested. It's saying goodbye to the promise. To what that project, that idea, that relationship could have been.

We were developing self-generating models for 3D crystals. We'd been at it for months. A lot of money invested. It worked. But AI was advancing at a speed that made our approach redundant before we finished. We could cling to the months invested, the sunk cost dressed up as commitment, which is the most dangerous argument there is. Or we could let go. We let go. The aces went into the muck. And it was the right call.

My self-criticism is harsh. It isn't a gentle voice suggesting improvements. It's feedback I didn't ask myself for, arriving unformatted and without anesthesia. It's loud. It's direct. And it has no interest in making me feel good. It's interested in making me see what I need to see. The building comes afterward, in reflection, when the blow has passed and what's left is clean information. But the first blow has no mercy.

Musashi also wrote: *"Today is victory over yourself of yesterday."* Choosing yourself isn't only moving forward. It's also knowing how to stop. Knowing when the hand you're holding is no longer the hand you need. Knowing when the version of you that made a decision six months ago is no longer the version of you sitting at this table. Clinging to the decisions of a dead version of you, one that had different information, different biases, different circumstances, is the subtlest way to stop choosing yourself. Because you're not choosing anymore. You're obeying someone who no longer exists.

---

### V. Being here

There's a trap almost all of us fall into: living under a series of future achievements as if the present were just the hallway you have to cross to get to the room that matters.

I'm not saying you shouldn't have goals. I'm saying that defining them in a way that blinds you, that makes you say no to everything that doesn't point straight at the target, is building a life in service of a version of you that may no longer exist. A dead version, clouded by other people's expectations, that one day decided you had to achieve something specific. On whose account? To get there and chase what? They're steps that don't lead to any floor. They lead to more steps.

Carrey said it better: *"You can spend your whole life imagining ghosts, worrying about the pathway to the future, but all there will ever be is what's happening here, and the decisions we make in this moment."*

I'm going to say something that sounds sad but in practice set me free: happiness the way it's sold doesn't exist. That permanent state you supposedly reach after achieving enough things, piling up enough successes, putting together the perfect life: it isn't there. It won't be. But what does exist, what I found when I stopped chasing it, are moments. Moments of genuine happiness, small and big, that show up when I'm present enough to see them. Not when I plan them. Not when I look for them. When I'm there. Happiness is the road, not the destination. And living, just living, is the real goal. Not arriving. Being.

Recently I went with some friends to FILBO, the Bogotá book fair. We saw their books. Some published a while ago, some launching that day. And what I felt wasn't abstract inspiration. It was pride. Concrete pride, with first and last names, for people I know and love. People who had worked for years for that moment to exist. And I was there. Not thinking about what I need to publish. Not comparing. Being there.

Another day, on the way to San Diego, looking out the plane window, I felt incredibly present and grateful. The day was beautiful. Colombia from above is absurd, with that geography that shouldn't exist. And in that moment I thought of all my friends, of all the people who make me feel like myself, and I wrote to them. Not for a date or a reason. Because I was there, looking out a window, feeling alive, and I wanted them to know.

Those moments are the happiness that does exist. And they don't show up while you're chasing the next step. They show up when you stop. When you quit running and let yourself be where you are. When you accept that the version of you that decided three years ago what you had to achieve meant well but had incomplete information. And that you, today, have every right to redefine what winning means.

Sometimes choosing yourself means choosing someone else. Being there with them. Holding them up. Being present so someone else can take their step without having to invent their universe alone. That doesn't contradict anything I've said in this series. It completes it. Because the end of independence isn't loneliness. It's the freedom to choose who you share what you built with.

---

Choosing yourself when it's easy isn't worth much. Everyone chooses themselves when the conditions are perfect.

Choosing yourself when it means saying no to someone hurts. Choosing yourself when it disappoints people you love hurts. Choosing yourself when you have no evidence it'll work, when the only guarantee is that if you don't, you'll be left wondering, hurts.

But Jim Carrey's father chose the safe option and lost anyway.

And the lesson isn't that safety doesn't exist. The lesson is that if you're going to lose, let it be with something you chose. Let the defeat be yours. Let the bet have been yours. So that when you look back, what you see isn't a life designed by fear disguised as practicality, but a life improvised with the tools you had, on the floor you were given, dancing without choreography but knowing the turns.

Sagan was right. To take the step, you have to invent the universe. But nobody tells you that inventing the universe is a job that never ends. That every morning you have to reinvent it a little. That yesterday's step doesn't work for today. And that the one you're taking now, with the hands you have, with the ingredients you found, with the recipe you're writing as you cook, is the only one that matters.
