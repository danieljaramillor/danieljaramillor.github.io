# Small Change

*On the most expensive word in the language, the small change I use to dodge it, and a debt I've gone years without paying*

I don't say sorry.

People who are just getting to know me take a while to notice, because nobody misses a word everyone gives away for free. But it's a fact, not a pose: the word barely comes out of me. I don't hand it out in elevators. I don't drop it on calls when the mic glitches. I don't use it to ask permission, or to interrupt, or to grease the day the way everyone does. And nobody has ever called me on it. Quite the opposite. The people who really know me understand the weight of the word precisely because they don't hear it from me often: when it comes out of me, something happened. It doesn't come lightly. And because it doesn't come lightly, when it does come, it doesn't need explaining. It arrives with the context already attached.

For years I read that as a virtue. I don't waste the word because I respect it: that was my theory, and it lasted as long as theories you never revisit do.

What I do say, all day long, like every Colombian, is *qué pena*. It's our all-purpose phrase, somewhere between "excuse me," "my bad," and "how embarrassing." *Qué pena con vos*. Sorry to bother you. Sorry for the delay. Sorry to keep pushing. That one comes out on its own, without going through the register, without touching the reserves. And in English, the language I spend half my working life in, I have the imported version: well, that sucks. Most of my day-to-day screw-ups, and I don't just mean the little ones, get settled with one of those two coins. That's the whole system, if you look at it from a distance: a man who hands out *qué penas* like flyers and keeps his apologies under lock and key.

For a long time that bookkeeping seemed flawless to me. It even gave me a quiet satisfaction, the satisfaction of a serious treasurer: small coins for daily friction, the big word in the vault, everything in its proper denomination. Until I realized where the vault was. And that keeping and owing look an awful lot alike.

---

### I. The census

The theory had to be tested, and I test things the only way I trust: by counting.

I started with a fifty-minute work call. Eleven sorries, none of them mine. Sorry to interrupt. Sorry, my mic froze. Sorry, go ahead. Sorry, can you hear me? Eleven times the word crossed the screen and eleven times there was nothing to forgive. Nobody had done any harm. Nobody expected an answer. Nobody stopped for a second to collect the payment: the conversation kept going straight, the way traffic keeps going after a honk.

And once you start counting, you can't stop. That's the problem with censuses: you get a taste for it. I kept going on the street: two in the line at the bakery, one in the elevator, from a man who pressed the button I'd already pressed, as if choosing the same floor were an offense that needed compensating. I kept going that night in WhatsApp voice notes: sorry for the long voice note, sorry for rambling, sorry for messaging so late. Sorries for existing near someone else. For occupying the same square meter. For breathing out of turn. The word circulates all day and buys nothing. A thing of beauty, all that waste.

Eleven sorries in fifty minutes and not a single harm on the table. That's not courtesy anymore. That's monetary policy.

Now you do the math, no tricks: how many times did you say sorry today? And how many of those had real harm behind them, harm the other person could have collected on? The distance between those two numbers is the inflation.

What happens to a word repeated that much has been measured in a lab, even though we all ran the experiment as kids without knowing it was one: repeating your own name until it sounded foreign, until it seemed like someone else's name, until it got a little scary. In 1962 a doctor in Montreal, Leon James, turned that game into science. He sat people down to repeat a word two or three times a second, with a stopwatch, and measured how long it took the word to die. Fifteen seconds. At fifteen seconds the sound was still intact and the meaning was gone, like a banknote with the number rubbed off. The explanation is physical before it's poetic: the neurons that respond to a word get fatigued if they're forced to fire without a break, and at some point they stop responding. The brain stops paying. James called it semantic satiation, and you don't have to take his word or mine: say *brick* forty times in a row and then tell me what's left in your mouth. A noise shaped like a word. A husk. James measured in a Montreal lab what I was counting in an elevator in Bogotá.

Now scale the experiment up. What a brain does in fifteen seconds, a culture does in a generation. We've been repeating sorry for decades, all of us, all the time, for nothing. In the elevator. On calls. At the bakery. In voice notes. Not two or three times a second, but two or three thousand times a lifetime, with most of them unbacked by any real harm. The word still sounds perfect. It sounds kind, it sounds polite, it sounds well brought up. What needs checking is how much it still means. Because words are currency, and a currency is worth something as long as there's something backing it, and eleven sorries without harm are eleven banknotes with no number. I wanted to know what backs this currency. So I went to ask where the reserves are supposed to be kept.

### II. The accountant

I went to the dictionary the way you go to a bank: to ask about the backing. I was looking for a word of the soul and I was met by an accountant.

The Royal Spanish Academy defines *perdonar*, to forgive, like this: to remit the debt, offense, fault, crime, or other thing. To remit the debt. That's the first sense, the main one, the one that comes before all the others. And the synonyms the entry for *perdón* offers follow the same line: remission, cancellation, pardon, amnesty. Bank paperwork and courthouse paperwork. The antonym it lists is punishment. In the whole definition there isn't a single feeling. There are balances, penalties, outstanding obligations. The dictionary has spent centuries telling us this is a matter of money, and we've spent centuries reading it as a matter of emotion. We're both right, but it got there first. English dictionaries say much the same: to forgive a debt is the oldest use of the verb in the book.

The etymology is even less sentimental. *Perdonar* comes from the Latin *perdonare*: *per*, an intensifier, and *donare*, to give. To give completely. A gift. English built the same word out of its own parts: *for-give*. And gifts have their own physics, which we all know as children even if we forget it as adults: they're asked for, not collected. Asking for a gift means accepting, in the same movement of the hand, that it can be refused. That the other person can look at your open hand and decide no. If it can't be refused, it isn't a gift. It's a tax. And nobody gets emotional paying taxes, or is transformed by it, or set loose from anything.

The definition also carries a note that looks like a formality and is a whole rulebook: said of the one who has been harmed by it. The verb has an owner. Only the injured party can conjugate it. The one who did the harm has no access to that verb no matter how much they want it, how much money they have, how many degrees they've earned: all they can do is ask, and wait on the outside of their own sentence. I like that asymmetry; it's raw. The language gave all the power to the person who took the hit. The offender was disarmed by design, reduced to asking. In a world that rewards whoever acts, executes, solves, the most expensive verb in the dictionary can only be executed by the one who stood still taking the damage.

Then I opened the student dictionary, the one the Academy publishes so kids learn how to use words, and looked up the same entry. The official example for *perdón*, the model they teach, is this: I apologize if I have offended you. I read it twice. Then I read it out loud, to see if it sounded different. It didn't. There, consecrated with an academic seal, is the same old trick. That conditional *if* turns my offense into your sensitivity. It shifts the burden of proof onto the injured party. It leaves open the possibility that nothing happened here at all and the problem is you, feeling too much, taking everything to heart. I'm not apologizing for what I did: I'm apologizing just in case you felt something. The language's central bank displays, as its sample banknote, a counterfeit. It honestly ruined my afternoon.

Hannah Arendt fixed it a little. Arendt saw the worst of the last century up close, had to flee her country for being who she was, and yet in 1958 she wrote one of the most generous paragraphs ever written about this word. Her starting point is a design problem: human beings act, and what we do can't be undone. Action is irreversible; it doesn't come with a reverse gear. I already wrote a whole essay about that, the one about the coffee you can't take back, so I came to this idea with the lesson learned: what's done is done, and there's no returning the cup. What I didn't know was that Arendt had found the problem a remedy, just one, and that the remedy was exactly the word I keep under lock and key. Forgiveness. The only human action capable of untying the knot of the past without denying it. It doesn't give anything back. It doesn't repair anything. It doesn't pretend it didn't happen. And yet it sets loose: it frees the one who did it and the one who received it from the chain of collections and revenge the harm had set in motion. Arendt noted something stranger still: that the discoverer of that power was Jesus of Nazareth. Not a Sunday-school teacher saying it. A secular Jewish philosopher, talking about politics, not faith. Forgiveness as a technology, not a miracle. A tool invented precisely because there's no reverse.

And she found it a twin: the promise. If forgiveness unties what already happened, the promise ties down what hasn't happened yet. Two technologies, one for each direction of time, and both run on the same fuel: words that are only worth something because they can be broken or refused. I read all that with the calm of someone studying someone else's problem. It didn't last long.

The same dictionary entry keeps an old sense almost nobody uses anymore. In the plural, *perdones* are the gifts a pilgrim brings back from a pilgrimage: the sweets, the dried fruit handed out on returning from the journey. The language knew it before I did. Forgiveness is a gift someone brings back from a long road. And gifts, once again, are asked for with open hands, or they aren't gifts.

### III. Petty cash

We solved the problem a long time ago and didn't even notice. Colombians invented fractional currency: the *qué pena*. *Qué pena con vos*. Sorry to bother you. Sorry for the delay. The *qué pena* doesn't ask anything of anyone. It doesn't request absolution. It doesn't open a case file. It doesn't wait for an answer. It declares my own embarrassment, puts it on the table, and keeps walking, all in one motion, without stopping. An instant, low-denomination payment that doesn't touch the reserves. That's why it can circulate all day without wearing out and without wearing out the big word. The language's petty cash. And the mechanism works because the other person answers in the same currency. *Qué pena con vos*. No, *qué pena* on my end. We both declare embarrassment, neither collects, and the debt dissolves in courtesy like sugar in a black coffee. It's a beautiful choreography. As long as the debts are the size of the choreography.

I live off that petty cash, and here's my first serious confession of this essay. Most of my day-to-day screw-ups, however big, get settled with a *qué pena*. I'm late to something you don't show up late to: sorry for the delay. I miss a message that mattered: sorry, it slipped through. I drop a commitment the other person had been waiting on for days: sorry, everything piled up this week. The coin comes out on its own and my hand doesn't even notice it paid.

And when the screw-up happens in English, I have the imported coin: well, that sucks. That one is worse, and it took me a while to see it. The *qué pena* at least has my face stamped on it: it declares my embarrassment even if it asks for nothing, it puts me in the scene, even if only in profile. "That sucks" doesn't have anyone's face on it. It has no debtor. No subject. No mourners. It's a weather report: some rain we're having, too bad about your project. I've closed out real harm with a weather report, and the other person has had to stand there, holding their harm, listening to me talk about the weather.

English, on top of that, doesn't even have real petty cash, and the consequences are plain to see. A language platform counted the uses of the British *sorry*: fifteen different ones, and only one of them means regret. The other fourteen are for asking permission, interrupting, squeezing past someone in a hallway, asking the time. A Brit says sorry nine times a day on average. A single word doing every job: the big coin forced to work as petty cash, day and night, until it's worn smooth. And the ending is already legal. In 2009 the province of Ontario, in Canada, passed a law literally called the Apology Act, which establishes that saying sorry does not constitute an admission of fault and can't be used as evidence in a civil trial. Think for a moment about what that means. An entire parliament, in full session, with reports and debates and a vote, legislating that the word no longer carries weight. That it can be said without counting. Inflation with an official seal, published in the gazette.

Our little coin, on the other hand, hides a past as a big one. *Pena* comes from the Latin *poena*, and *poena* from the Greek *poinē*, and *poinē*, in Homer, wasn't a feeling. It was money. The fine a killer paid the dead man's family, the assessed price of a life. In the *Iliad* the scene is engraved, literally engraved, on Achilles' shield: two men arguing in the public square over what a spilled life is worth, with the townspeople around them taking sides, and the word at the center of the dispute is that one. *Poinē*. Blood money. From there it slid down to punishment, from punishment to suffering, and from suffering, on this side of the world, to embarrassment. English took the same coin and minted *penalty*, *penance*, and *pain* out of it. When I say *qué pena con vos*, I'm using, without knowing it, a word that was born counting money over a corpse. The little coin is, literally, a coin. It's been one for three thousand years.

So the system is perfect and the flaw is me. Having petty cash isn't the problem: it's pure economics, daily friction paid in cash and on the spot, no paperwork, no drama, no turning every brush into a courtroom. The problem is what I've done with it. Because I've paid big debts with *qué penas* and real harm with "that sucks." I've hurt people for real, the kind of hurt that leaves a mark, and I've swiped the small-regrets card hoping the card reader wouldn't notice the difference. The other person always notices. Always. They might not say anything; they might even answer *qué pena con vos*, don't worry about it; but they notice. I thought I was paying, and what I was doing was putting my stinginess on record.

### IV. Questions that don't ask

Questions are currency too, and the one I've counterfeited the most always starts the same way. I read someone else's paragraph that I couldn't have written, the kind that keeps ringing in your head for the rest of the day. Or I watch someone pull off on the dance floor, without rehearsing, what takes me whole seasons. Or I come across a finished piece of work with that ease that's almost offensive.

My head fires off the usual voice: why can't I do that? Why don't I write like that? Why can't I get there?

For years I thought that sentence was a question because it ends in a question mark. It's a lie. Nobody who says it to themselves sits down afterward to investigate the answer. Nobody opens a notebook. Nobody lists causes. Nobody interviews the party involved. The sentence doesn't wait for an answer because it doesn't need one: it comes with the verdict inside, already decided. "Why can't I" is a sentence handed down in a questioning tone, and the proof is in what it leaves behind. It doesn't leave a task. It leaves a mood. You say it and you don't go off to find anything out; you go off to feel smaller. I recognize it by the gesture that goes with it: I close the tab, turn my phone face down, get up for a coffee. The fake question doesn't produce data; it produces escape moves. Real questions are recognized by the opposite, by the trail of work they leave behind.

"How" is a different species. "How does she do it" already forces you to look. It forces you to stay in the discomfort a little longer, with the tab open, with someone else's work in front of you. But right there comes the second trap, a subtler one I've also fallen into: believing that the answer to "how" is copying the other person's process. It doesn't work, and it doesn't work for a reason I've already had to write about in this series: work that makes the hard look easy is invisible. What I see of the other person is the husk, the final gesture, the result with the lights on. The hours aren't in the photo. The drafts aren't in the photo. The versions thrown away, the years behind closed doors, the times it went wrong and nobody saw: none of that is in the photo. And what can't be observed can't be copied. Whoever imitates the visible is imitating the costume.

So the useful question isn't how they do it.

It's how I get to that result, with my materials, by my route.

Not "Why can't I...?" but "How do I...?"

The other person isn't a map. They're a road sign: proof that the place exists. That's as far as their job goes.

I should know this better than anyone, because my résumé has no map. I went from industrial design to oncology, from oncology to crypto, from crypto to artificial intelligence, and on none of those jumps was there someone else's route to copy: nobody had made that exact trip with my exact materials, and the people who knew the destination knew nothing about my starting point. Every time, the only question available was how do I get there. And even with that track record, the fake voice still goes off inside me when I read someone else's paragraph. Knowing the grammar doesn't exempt you from speaking badly.

Read that way, even envy changes jobs. What burns in me in front of someone else's paragraph isn't information about them: it's information about me, and with a precision no career test has. Not just anything burns. Cars don't burn. Titles don't burn. Followers don't burn. Two or three very specific things burn, always the same ones, and those two or three things are the most honest inventory I have of what I want. Envy as a verdict poisons. Envy as an instrument points, by first and last name, without the diplomacy I lie to myself with the rest of the day.

And "how" has an extra honesty that "why" will never have: sometimes the answer is a price, and a price can be turned down. Asking how can hand you back a number. Ten years. A certain sacrifice. So much skin. And saying "I won't pay that price" is also a grown-up answer, maybe the most grown-up of all, because it's the opposite of "I can't." "I can't" is a verdict without a trial. "I won't pay it" is a decision with the bill in plain view. They look alike from the outside and are enemies on the inside.

There's a third question left, and that one doesn't compare anything. After the "why" that condemns and the "how" that investigates comes the one you answer alone: and what does this mean to me? Not what the dictionary says. Not what Ontario legislated. Not what blood was worth in Homer. What asking forgiveness means to me. And the answer I find has the same grammar as real questions. Asking forgiveness, for me, is handing the other person the right to refuse me. Putting the no in their hand and standing still while they decide, without rushing the verdict, without helping them say yes. A real question accepts any answer. A real apology accepts the no. They're the same animal wearing a different collar.

### V. The teller's window

There's an apology I owe. I won't say to whom, and it isn't to protect anyone: it's that I'm not opening that door yet, not even here. But I have it located the way you locate a debt. I know the amount. I know since when. I know where the teller's window is. I've spent years paying around it: well-timed kindnesses, favors nobody asked for, messages on the important dates, *qué penas* of every caliber. Interest. Whole years of pure interest, and the principal untouched. The debtor who pays his interest on time is the most comfortable character in the world: nobody chases him, nobody garnishes his wages, people even think he's a decent guy. And the debt never goes down.

I know why I haven't gone. Really paying means saying the whole sentence, without the trick *if*, without the escape-hatch *but*, looking them in the face, and standing still. And that person can say no. Their no reaches me. It gets all the way to where I live. The elevator sorries don't cost anything because a stranger's no doesn't touch anyone: handing out currency that doesn't hurt isn't paying, it's making the noise of payment. I don't make that noise, it's true. But I found out my silence wasn't payment either.

Because the people who know me say that when the word comes out of me, it carries weight. And for years I settled into that prestige. Having a reputation as hard currency is delicious: you walk differently knowing your signature is worth something, that you don't go around giving away the ink. Until I understood the difference between a coin that weighs and a coin that pays. Mine weighs in the vault, and in there it doesn't pay anyone anything: a coin that never leaves is worth the same as a worn-out one. Inflation and burial end up at the same counter. Everything I haven't said to that person I haven't held back to protect the word. I've held it back so I wouldn't have to stand there with my hand open, waiting on a verdict I don't control.

That's what this essay was, I see it now. I spent four sections counting other people's coins, the sorries on a call, the English *sorry*, the Greeks' fine, so I wouldn't have to count mine. It's an old trick and it came out naturally: whoever audits everyone else looks solvent.

The word is worn out; that's been proven, doctorate and all. And the wear gave me a double alibi. If the currency's no longer worth anything, why pay with it? And if mine is still worth something, better not spend it. Both lies. The word is worth exactly what you risk by saying it. Said on a call over a frozen mic, it's worth pennies. Said with the other person's no on the table, it's worth everything in the vault.

One of these days I'm going to that window with the whole sentence and open hands. They might come back empty. That they can come back empty is the only proof I went to pay and not to make more noise. The debt is still there. Now I know what pays it.
