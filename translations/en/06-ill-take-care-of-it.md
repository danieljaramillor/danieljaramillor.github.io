# I'll Take Care of It

*On expecting nothing, remembering everything, and the shadow that falls between the two*

I don't expect anything from anyone. Ever. From anyone.

Most people who hear that assume something happened to me. That someone failed me in some unfixable way and this is the scar. That behind the sentence there's a story of betrayal, of deep disappointment, of broken trust. That it's a wall. A defense.

It isn't.

It's older than any wound and simpler than it sounds. It's a design decision. The same one that led me to build a parallel curriculum in high school because no class was teaching me what I wanted to learn. The same one that led me to start a company in my third semester of university while keeping up with the official coursework. Talking with the dean recently, he put it in words that stuck with me: it's as if you'd had two curricula, the one they gave you and the one you chose with your life. That's exactly what I did. And it's exactly what I'm still doing.

If I want something, it's on me. Not as a complaint. Not as resentment. As architecture.

---

### I. The architecture of not expecting

There's a difference between needing people and depending on them. The first is a human reality there's no point denying. The second is an emotional architecture that produces fragility. When your mood rises or falls depending on whether someone texted you, when your day is ruined because an expectation didn't come through, what you're experiencing isn't connection. It's dependence. And expecting someone to prioritize your well-being with the same intensity you do is asking them to be you. Nobody is you. Nobody's going to be you.

What I learned in Germany, which I told in another piece of this series, cemented that conviction in a way I wouldn't have chosen but wouldn't change. When everything collapses and all that's left is you, your tools, and your ability to decide what to do with what's there, you discover something you can't learn any other way: the foundation has to be you. Not because other people don't matter. Because if the foundation isn't you, when other people fail, and they will, because they're human, there's nothing left to stand on.

You see it more in everyday life than in crises. You see it on any old Tuesday when a supplier doesn't answer and, instead of waiting, I find the alternative before the silence can stall me. You see it when a plan falls through and, instead of getting frustrated with whoever canceled, I'm already putting together plan B. You see it when someone doesn't do what they said and my first reaction isn't disappointment but recalibration: okay, what's next? It isn't coldness. It's that the system no longer depends on the other person following through. It works the same either way. What changes is the route, not the destination.

But it isn't only professional. In my personal life it shows up in subtler ways that are sometimes harder to explain. It shows up when someone says "let's do such-and-such next week" and my brain doesn't even register the promise as a promise. It registers it as an intention, which is different. An intention is what the person feels at the moment they say it. A promise is what they're supposed to deliver afterward. I keep the intention and let go of the promise. If it happens, amazing. If not, nothing lost, because I never counted on it. That lets me be in relationships with a lightness that's natural to me but can be confusing to others: how does it not get to you? How does it not bother you? It's not that it doesn't get to me. It's that I've processed the information before feelings can contaminate it. And the information, clean, almost always says the same thing: that person meant well and life got in the way. There's no malice. There's humanity.

People who know me well notice it. Some admire it. Some find it disconcerting. And I understand both reactions, because living this way takes an emotional discipline that isn't natural. You train it. You practice it. You choose it every day.

And something unexpected comes out of that position: when you expect nothing from anyone, everything that comes is a gift. Every act of generosity, every time someone thinks of you without being asked, lands with a different weight. It isn't an obligation being met. It's a free act. And free acts, the ones born without debt or expectation, are the ones that mean the most. You can't buy that, negotiate it, or schedule it. It comes or it doesn't. And when it comes, you feel it.

The other thing that comes from not expecting is clarity. When you have no expectations about what the other person will do, whatever they do or don't do arrives as pure data. It doesn't pass through the filter of disappointment, which is just an unmet expectation. And from that clarity you make better decisions about who gets your time and energy. About who deserves to be at the center of your life and who's fine at the edges.

Alain de Botton says it better than I can: mature love is seeing the other person as they are, not as you want them to be, and choosing to stay. That clear-sightedness doesn't only apply to partners. It applies to friends, to business partners, to the reality you were dealt. Expecting nothing from anyone isn't the same as no longer caring. It's caring with your eyes open.

But that clarity casts a shadow. And it took me a while to see it.

### II. Attention as a way of loving

We live in an era where attention is the most valuable currency there is, and at the same time the one we manage worst. Everything is designed to fragment it. The infinite scroll. Notifications. The conversation interrupted to check the phone. We give our presence to algorithms that don't deserve it and withhold it from people who do.

Byung-Chul Han diagnosed it precisely in *The Burnout Society*: deep attention is going extinct. Multitasking is celebrated as a virtue when it's actually a regression. Animals in the wild multitask because they need to watch for predators while they eat. Sustained, directed concentration is what sets us apart. And we're giving it away for cheap dopamine. Han argues that an excess of stimuli doesn't make us more productive. It makes us shallower. It turns us into consumers of fragments that never assemble into anything whole. And what's lost in the process isn't efficiency. It's depth. The ability to stay with something long enough to really understand it.

In that context, the act of really paying attention, listening and retaining, connecting something someone said six months ago with something tangible today, has become almost a form of resistance. Not because it's heroic. Because it's rare. And because when someone shows they were listening, that they kept hold of something that seemed insignificant, it feels like an act of affection. And it is. Maybe the purest one there is, because you can't fake it. Either you were listening or you weren't. There's no shortcut. No hack. No way to pretend you remember something you never took in.

I don't take notes. Ever. Not in meetings, not in conversations, not in almost anything. I trained my brain for a long time to retain as much as possible, and at some point I developed a conviction I still hold: if I don't retain it, it wasn't important. I lose details, of course. I'm human. But that training gave me something I didn't expect: a way of being present that forces you to really listen.

Something happens when you decide not to take notes: you run out of excuses. You can't trust the paper to remember for you. What goes in has to stay under its own weight, and whatever doesn't stay is something your brain decided wasn't worth it. That filter, uncomfortable at first, over time becomes a brutal prioritization tool. Your brain learns to tell what matters from what doesn't. Not at the level of hard data, which you can always look up, but at the level of nuance. The emotions behind what someone said. The thing they mentioned in passing that reveals something about who they are. The details people forget they shared. That's what stays. And that's what makes the difference between listening and being present.

Borges wrote "Funes the Memorious" about a man who remembered absolutely everything and for whom that was a curse. Thinking requires forgetting. Abstraction requires letting go of details to see patterns. I'm not Funes. My memory isn't total. But I know the tension Borges describes. Sometimes I remember plans we made that only I remember. Ideas thrown out in a conversation, half-made promises that were thin air to the other person and an archive entry to me. And I know bringing it up can make people uncomfortable. As if by remembering something the other person forgot, I'm pointing at an imbalance better left alone. So I bring it up in subtle ways, or I don't. I negotiate with my own memory so I don't make the other person feel bad for forgetting something that stuck with me.

Wong Kar-wai understood something about this. In *In the Mood for Love*, the entire relationship between the two leads happens in what goes unsaid. In the details each notices about the other without mentioning them. In the silent dedication that isn't announced and doesn't ask for recognition. There's a scene where the two walk side by side, almost brushing, and what the viewer understands comes not from what they say but from what they chose to notice about each other without saying it. That film is a visual essay on what it means to really pay attention to someone: not telling them you are. Just doing it. And letting them discover it on their own. Or not. Because the act is worth the same whether they see it or not.

And there's something worth saying here: memory in relationships is almost never symmetrical. You carry a version of the other person that includes everything they ever told you, and they carry a version of you that might include nothing you told them. That isn't unfair. It's just how people work. Everyone retains what their brain decides to retain, and that has nothing to do with how much they love you and everything to do with how they process. But living on the side of the one who remembers more has a particular texture. It's carrying an archive of someone that they don't know exists. And learning not to resent that difference, not to turn it into evidence that you matter less to them, is part of what it means to give without expecting anything back.

Sometimes I'm on a trip, in a shop, at a market, somewhere that has nothing to do with anyone I know, and I see something that reminds me of someone. An object, a book, a piece that connects to something that person said once. And I buy it. Sometimes I save it for the right moment, a birthday, whenever it feels right to give it. And sometimes I don't. Sometimes I'm impatient and give it to them the next day, or that same week, for no reason, no date, no excuse. Because the connection between the object and the person is so clear that waiting feels artificial. Giving just because, with no occasion to justify it, is another way of saying "I thought of you, and I don't need a reason to show you."

Other times I do accumulate. I keep things from different trips, different moments, objects that turned up in different cities and that connect to the same person. And when the time comes, I give them everything at once. Not a gift. An accumulation of moments when I was somewhere in the world and that person was with me in my head. In three different cities, at three moments that had nothing to do with each other, something reminded me of you. And each of those times, I kept it. It isn't something I do with everyone or all the time. But when I do, what I'm handing over isn't a set of objects. It's proof that what you told me didn't evaporate.

Kundera wrote in *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* that every life is lived only once and therefore can't be rehearsed. Everything is a final draft, everything is a first take; there's no rough copy. Every conversation is unique. Every detail someone shares with you is an unrepeatable act of trust. And the decision to remember it or let it go isn't trivial. It's a way of telling the other person: what you told me mattered enough to stay.

I hardly expect anyone to recognize the effort. What I hope, if I hope for anything, is that it's my baseline. That the people who know me know that's how I work. I don't need it returned. I need them to know it's genuine.

And when someone does the same for me, when someone remembers a detail of mine, something I mentioned in passing, it reaches me deeper than any gift. Remembering is the gift. Everything else is logistics.

### III. What the pho taught me

One day I decided to make pho for my friends. Real pho. Bone broth that takes hours, toasted spices, the whole process. I confirmed attendance that morning. Nine people. I started cooking at two in the afternoon. Bones, charred onion, ginger, star anise, cinnamon, clove. You bring it to a slow simmer and let it do its work for hours while the apartment fills with a smell that tells you something good is happening. A plan made with love and with the care I put into the things that matter to me.

At six in the evening, when the broth had been going for four hours, I was the one who had to text them. To ask if they were coming. Because nobody had said a word all day.

They dropped off one by one. Of nine, there were four of us left, counting my sister and me. I served the pho. We ate a lot. It came out good. I froze what was left in Tupperware, just the broth, because I'd made the noodles and the meat in exactly the right amount for the people who were there.

And in the moment, I felt nothing. My brain processed the situation with its usual logic: it's their nature, I'm not surprised, I wasn't expecting anything, the food freezes, nothing's wasted. Next.

It was later, days later, that I understood it had hurt.

Not the flaking itself. People bail every day and life goes on. What hurt was realizing that my system of expecting nothing had kept me from registering the pain while it was happening. That I'd filed the experience in the wrong folder: I'd put it under "things that happen" when it belonged under "things that matter."

De Botton has an idea that strikes me as one of the most uncomfortable and true things about relationships: the person who loves you most is the one who will disappoint you most, because they're the closest and therefore have the most chances to let you down. A stranger who cancels on you doesn't move the needle. A close friend who cancels after you've spent four hours cooking for them is something else. The difference isn't the cancellation. It's which layer of the onion that person lives in.

I talked to them about it afterward. Not as a complaint. As information. And that conversation taught me there are relationships where reciprocity isn't a luxury. It's a necessity. You can't treat the person in the outer layer the same as the person at the center. There are people who are still in my archive but no longer have the keys to the building. Not out of resentment. Because time is finite, and I decided to invest it where the return is mutual.

Expecting nothing from anyone and giving with the dedication I give are two forces that live in permanent tension. You expect nothing, but you cook for four hours. You expect nothing, but you pick gifts with the precision of someone who's been listening for months. You expect nothing, but you archive every detail of the people you love. And if I don't expect anything back, where does the pain come from when there's no reciprocity? The honest answer is that expecting nothing doesn't mean feeling nothing. It means the protective mechanism processes things before the feeling arrives. And sometimes it processes so fast the feeling gets lost on the way. It doesn't disappear. It gets filed in the wrong folder. And there it stays until a Tupperware of pho in the freezer forces you to open it.

But I want to be honest about something: not every signal means the same thing. Sometimes not feeling pain is exactly the right response. Sometimes someone cancels on you and it genuinely doesn't matter, and the ability not to give weight to what doesn't deserve it is a real strength. Not everything deserves pain. Not everything deserves a reaction. The problem isn't that the mechanism exists. It's that sometimes it doesn't sort well. Sometimes it files a "thing that matters" as a "thing that happens." What's missing isn't sensitivity. It's calibration.

### IV. Building

Murakami's protagonists are guys who function alone with an almost elegant efficiency. Toru Watanabe in *Norwegian Wood*, Tengo in *1Q84*. They build routines, spaces, inner worlds that don't need outside validation to exist. They cook for themselves. They read alone. They walk alone. And there's something beautiful in that self-sufficiency, something anyone who's learned to be okay with themselves recognizes. The freedom of not needing anyone for the day to work. The calm of knowing that if the plan falls apart, you have the tools to build another. The certainty that your well-being isn't in anyone else's hands.

But Murakami never leaves the story there. His characters discover, always late, that self-sufficiency has a cost: a functional loneliness that feels fine until one day it feels empty. That the independence that protected them from chaos also isolated them from something they needed without knowing they needed it. Murakami never judges them. He shows them. And what he shows is that radical independence isn't an end state. It's a starting point that needs something more to become a full life. Not less independence. More connection, built from that independence. Which isn't the same as dependence. It's choice.

Chihiro, in Miyazaki's *Spirited Away*, doesn't wait for anyone to save her. She's ten years old, alone in a world she doesn't understand, and what she does is dive in, learn the rules, work, solve. Not because she's brave in the epic sense, but because she understands something many adults don't: waiting for someone else to fix your situation is the surest way to stay exactly where you are. What makes that film great isn't the magic. It's a girl who decides her situation is her responsibility and acts accordingly. That, at ten or at thirty, is the hardest and most freeing thing there is.

That's what I try to do every day. If something blocks me, I look for the other door. And if there's no other door, I ask myself whether I can knock down a wall. It doesn't always work. Sometimes the wall won't budge. Sometimes the door you find leads somewhere worse than where you were. But the decision to act instead of wait is what makes the difference between living a life you chose and living one you ended up with by default.

And building isn't something you do once. It's something you choose every morning. I wake up and there's a decision to make before all the others, one that has nothing to do with work or my calendar or whatever happened the day before: I choose what the day is going to be like. Not the circumstances; I don't control those. My stance toward them. I choose to cook even if I don't know who's coming. I choose to send someone something because I thought of them, just because. I choose to sit down and write a four-thousand-word essay that maybe three people will read, and I write it anyway because I need to think it through out loud. I choose not to let my mood be set by a message that did or didn't arrive. That isn't optimism. It's a practice. Like training a muscle that won't respond at first and over time becomes automatic, until one day you realize you're no longer choosing to be happy. You just are, because you built the conditions for it.

I don't do anything looking for reciprocity. I don't do anything looking for approval. I don't cook to be thanked. I don't give gifts to get them back. I don't write these pieces to be applauded. Happiness that depends on what other people do is happiness held hostage. It's anxiety with good manners. The kind I'm after is the kind I build myself. And if the day goes badly, I know I can change it. And if it goes well, I know it wasn't luck. It was construction.

---

I know there's a contradiction in all this. I expect nothing from anyone, but I give everything. I don't look for reciprocity, but the pho hurt. I don't need approval, but I write essays I publish so people will read them. I'm not going to resolve it. I don't want to. The contradiction is what makes this human and not an operating system. The day I stop giving more than I expect to receive is the day I stopped being me.

Most people live waiting. Waiting for the right moment, the right opportunity, the right person, the right conditions. Waiting for someone else to cook.

Next time I make pho, I'm going to cook for nine. Because that's how I cook. And if four show up, it'll still be good. The broth will be ready at six either way, because the person who made it wasn't waiting to see who came before deciding whether it was worth it.

I'll take care of it. I always have. And honestly, I wouldn't know how to live any other way.
