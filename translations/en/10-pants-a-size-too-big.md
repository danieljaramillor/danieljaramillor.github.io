# Pants a Size Too Big

*On the anxiety that lives in the calm, the noise that doesn't need you, and why being enough doesn't require doing more*

Sometimes I'm in a meeting with people who have twenty years more experience than I do, people who've spent decades in their field, who speak with the confidence of someone who's already made every possible mistake, and I think: at some point they're going to figure it out. I don't know what exactly. That I don't belong. That I'm improvising. That if you asked me how I got here, the honest answer is that some days I don't know either. That the pants I'm wearing are too big for me and I've spent years covering it up with speed. Moving so fast nobody notices the belt is on its last notch and they're still slipping.

Pants a size too big. That's what I call it, inside. I've never said it out loud, because saying it out loud means admitting something that doesn't fit the image you project when you're at the front of a room, a team, a project. But it's there. Every time I look at what I've built and then look at my friends my age, I feel the extra fabric. Not because I haven't worked to fill them. Because the pants I put on belong to someone who should be bigger than I am. I put them on myself. Nobody picked them for me. And sometimes, when the noise dies down and the calm sets in, you look down and feel how huge they are on you. And that if you stop moving, they'll fall down.

What I've never said in this series, and need to say now in so many words, is that my anxiety doesn't live where people think it does. It doesn't show up when I've got a thousand things going. It doesn't show up in the chaos, in the fourteen-meeting week, in the impossible deadline, in the project that's on fire. That's where I function. That's where my brain lights up and does what it knows how to do: solve, prioritize, move pieces, grit my teeth and keep going. There I'm fine. There the pants fit, because you don't have time to look down.

The anxiety shows up when things are calm.

When the workload drops. When there isn't much to do. When the chaos clears and there's peace. And in that peace, in that stillness that should feel like rest, a voice switches on that has no face but plenty of volume: you're not adding value. You should be doing something. Why are you sitting still? Move. Produce. Justify your presence. Because if you don't justify it, who will?

It's not that I don't like being alone. It's not that I don't enjoy those moments. That's when I travel, read, explore things that have nothing to do with work. But the anxiety is still there in the background, like a bass line that never stops. The need to contribute. To fill the pants. To prove I deserve the space I take up. Not to anyone in particular. To myself. Which is infinitely worse. Because you can negotiate with a boss. You can argue with a client. With yourself there's no negotiating. You know exactly where the gaps are.

Jorge Drexler has a song called "Silencio." Silence is the loudest noise. That idea follows me around because it describes, with clinical accuracy, what happens to me. My anxiety isn't in the noise. It's in the silence. When everything goes quiet, when nothing is urgent, when the phone doesn't buzz and the calendar is empty and nobody needs me for anything in particular, that's when it shows up. Not as a panic attack. Not as a crisis. As a weight on the chest that won't lift. As the stubborn feeling that standing still is failing. Anyone who makes a living creating something (songs, companies, essays, whatever) knows that vertigo. The silence between one creation and the next isn't rest. It's the question of whether another one will come. Whether what you did before was enough or was luck. Whether the pants you put on were the right ones, or look ridiculous on you and you just haven't noticed yet.

### I. The deafening jungle

Recently I stopped. Really stopped. I went traveling in Colombia, to a tiny town with rivers everywhere, one of those towns where people still say hello without knowing you and time is measured in meals, not meetings. We went canyoning, which is basically getting into the river and letting it carry you between rocks, waterfalls, pools you don't know how deep they are until you're in them. The ice-cold water. The roar of the current bouncing off granite. Your body reacting to something that isn't a screen or an email or a deadline but raw nature telling you: hey, you're not in charge here. Here your title doesn't matter, or your company, or your pants. Here you're a guy in a river, and the river takes you wherever it feels like.

Afterward I went to a granite canyon where you sleep on a balcony at the edge of the jungle. Literally: a balcony hanging off the cliff, looking out over the treetops. Howler monkeys wake you up. Not an alarm, not notifications, not the seven a.m. email. The monkeys. Screaming at the top of their lungs from the trees as if sunrise were an emergency that had to be announced. And you there, on a balcony, with a coffee someone made you without being asked, watching the mist lift through the trees and thinking about absolutely nothing. Nothing. A luxury I hadn't allowed myself in years. A luxury I was afraid to allow myself, because thinking about nothing, for someone who defines himself by always thinking about something, feels dangerously close to not existing.

At night, the jungle is deafening. That's the exact word. Deafening. Thousands of insects, night birds, frogs you can't see but that sound like they're right next to you, the river in the background doing its thing. A constant, dense, enveloping noise that wraps around you without asking. There's no silence in the jungle. Not a single second of it. Everything is alive. Everything is doing something. But nothing it's doing has anything to do with you. And that, which sounds obvious, is devastating once you realize what it means: the world works perfectly without your involvement. The jungle doesn't need you. The monkeys don't know you exist. All that noise is proof of life, just not yours. And there you are, standing on a balcony, thinking the universe was waiting for you.

One night I stayed up on that balcony. Not doing anything. Just listening. Trying to tell the sounds apart: which was an insect, which a bird, which the river, which something I couldn't even name. And at some point I stopped trying to tell them apart and let myself be wrapped up in it. The noise wasn't noise. It was the jungle being the jungle. No conductor, no score, nobody evaluating it or optimizing it or trying to make it more efficient. And in the middle of all that noise that didn't need me for anything at all, the anxiety went away.

Not gradually. Not with effort. It went. From the second day it was gone. Knowing I was completely disconnected, that there was no signal, that I couldn't open the chat or check email or find out about anything even if I wanted to, was magic. I use that word without irony. As if my brain had been waiting for permission to switch off, and when the permission finally came, it grabbed it with both hands and didn't let go.

There was one moment of anxiety during the trip; some things happened that were out of my control. But that was different. That anxiety I identified, processed, took back control of what I could control, and it went away. It wasn't the one in my chest. It wasn't the too-big-pants one. It was anxiety with a cause. The kind that shows up because something real happened and goes away when you do something real about it. Not the kind that shows up because nothing is happening and doesn't go away because there's nothing to do. That distinction, between anxiety with a concrete cause and anxiety that comes out of the void, is the most important thing I discovered on that trip. And I didn't discover it by thinking. I discovered it by stopping thinking.

Meanwhile, back in Bogotá, everything kept running. No team collapsed. No process stalled. All the material I'd prepared was there to handle whatever came up. Everything worked without me. And that says more about what I built than about what I'm worth when I'm there. But knowing it and feeling it are two completely different things. The brain understands. The chest doesn't read arguments.

### II. The cathedral

Ken Follett wrote *The Pillars of the Earth* about a mason named Tom Builder who wants to build a cathedral. Not a house. Not a bridge. A cathedral. The biggest, most ambitious, most absurdly out-of-proportion work anyone could dream of in medieval England. A project of decades. One that would outlive whoever started it. That required a faith in the process bordering on madness. And what drives Tom isn't fame or money or recognition. It's the need to build. To always have a project in hand. A stone to set. A problem to solve. A Monday morning with something to do. Without that, Tom falls apart. Literally. He walks the roads of England with his family, without work, without direction, looking for a building site. Any site. Because without something to build, Tom doesn't know who he is. His identity is the building. Without it he's a man with tools and no purpose. In pants that are way too big, with nobody to tell him he put them on himself.

I don't like how much of myself I see in Tom. It isn't comfortable to admit that your identity depends on always having something on the go. That when every front is quiet, when there's no stone to set and no fire to put out, you don't feel peace. You feel a specific emptiness. Not the existential kind. Not the philosophical kind. The builder's kind, getting up on a Monday with nothing to build. The emptiness that tells you that if you're not producing, you're wasting the day. That if there's no problem, you'd better find one. That the pants won't fill themselves.

A couple of days ago, just getting back into work after the trip, I sat down at my desktop and discovered that at one of my jobs there was nothing pending. Nothing. The day was free. Free to explore, to look for things to improve, to think calmly. And instead of enjoying it, I felt the anxiety rise from my chest. The familiar one. The usual one. The one that says: if you're not producing, you're not contributing. If there's no emergency, go find one.

I opened a tab. Closed it. Opened another. Looked for an email to answer. Nothing urgent. Checked the pipelines. All in order. Looked at the dashboards. All green. And every green screen, every indicator in order, every sign that things were fine made me feel worse. Because my operating system isn't designed for green. It's designed for red. For the problem. For the missing stone. All green should mean "good job." To me it means "they don't need you." And yes, I know how absurd that sounds. But there it was. Sitting at a computer with everything in order, feeling like I was failing.

The most ironic part is that the jungle had shown me, just days before, that everything works without me. That systems run, people operate, cathedrals don't fall down because the mason takes a few days off. And still, sitting in front of the green screen, the anxiety came back. Because it's one thing to understand something watching the mist from a balcony and quite another to live it on a Tuesday at ten a.m. with an empty calendar and hands that don't know what to do. There's no mist there. There's a screen that's all green and a chest telling you green is the wrong color.

Tom Builder died before he saw the cathedral finished. Others finished it. With his plans, his foundations, the structure he designed. The cathedral wasn't Tom's. It outlived him. But confusing the need for the process with the need to be indispensable is the trap those of us who can't stop fall into. The trap of believing that if you're not setting a stone, the cathedral falls down. It doesn't. It never does. Because if you built it right, what you left behind is stronger than your presence.

### III. The shadow

Phil Stutz (the therapist in Jonah Hill's documentary) says anxiety is the shadow of growth. Not the obstacle. The shadow. If you grow, the shadow grows. If you stay still, the shadow stays the same size. Comfortable. Predictable. And dead. Trying to eliminate anxiety is trying to stop growing. And trying to stop growing is lying to yourself.

Jiro Ono is over eighty and still feels anxious before every service. Six decades making sushi. His chest still tightens when he opens the doors of his ten-seat restaurant in Tokyo. It hasn't gone away. He didn't "get over it." It's still there. Because anxiety isn't a sign something's wrong. It's a sign he still cares enough to get nervous. The day Jiro stops feeling it is the day he should retire.

There's something freeing in that idea if you let it in without fighting it. It's not about eliminating anxiety. It's about understanding which anxiety is yours and which is borrowed. Because not all anxiety is the same, and mixing them up is what destroys you.

The kind that's yours, generated by things within your control, your reach, your responsibility, has a shape. It has edges. You can pick it up, look it in the eye, understand where it comes from, and do something about it. It's fuel. It's Jiro's before service. It's Tom's as he sets the first stone without knowing whether he'll see the last. It's what pushes you to prepare the material that holds the team up when you're not there. That anxiety isn't the problem. It's the tool.

But there's another kind. The kind generated by something external. Something you don't control, that doesn't depend on you, that just happens or that someone else decides. That kind has no edges. It seeps through the cracks. It settles in your chest like damp in an old wall. And worst of all: it disguises itself as the first kind. It passes itself off as your responsibility, your problem, your load. But it isn't yours. And carrying anxiety that isn't yours is like putting on pants you didn't even choose and killing yourself trying to fill them.

When anxiety shows up, the first thing I do now (and I say this as someone still learning, not someone who's solved it) is ask: is this mine? Was what I'm feeling caused by something I can change, something that's in my court? If the answer is yes, the anxiety turns into action. It becomes moving a piece, solving something, taking control of what I can control.

But if the answer is no, if what I'm feeling is caused by something that doesn't depend on me, something I can't move, then it isn't mine. It's borrowed. And I have to let it go before it settles in my chest and stays there pretending to be mine. Because borrowed anxiety is the kind that gets the upper hand. The kind that piles up uninvited. The kind that puts you in pants that aren't yours and tells you that you have to fill them.

Stutz says something else I find brutal: that people spend their lives looking for a state where everything is fine. No anxiety, no discomfort, no pressure. A state of permanent peace where the pants fit perfectly and the chest doesn't tighten. And that state doesn't exist. It's not that you haven't found it. It doesn't exist. What exists is learning to move with the shadow. To operate with it there. To stop fighting something that won't leave and start using it for what it is: evidence that you're alive, that you care, that what you do has weight. No weight, no shadow. And no shadow means you're not growing.

Jiro makes sushi with anxiety. Tom sets stones with anxiety. Drexler writes songs knowing the silence will come back between one and the next. And I write essays with a tight chest and my hands on the keyboard, knowing the anxiety won't leave. That it never leaves. That the pants will always feel too big. And that the day they stop feeling too big is the day you stopped growing into them.

### IV. Enough

The day with nothing pending wasn't solved by an epiphany. I didn't open a tab and find the answer. What I did was something that for me is harder than solving any crisis: I stayed with the discomfort. I didn't fight it. I didn't look for an artificial task to fill the void. I didn't open Google Chat looking for a fire to put out. I didn't invent a crisis to feel useful.

I just sat there. Chest tight. Pants loose. The voice saying I should be doing something. And instead of obeying it, I listened to it. Not to do what it said. To see its face.

And what I found behind the voice wasn't an order. It was fear. Raw fear, undisguised. Fear that if you're not producing, you're not contributing. Fear that if everything works without you, then you're surplus. Fear that the pants you chose are bigger than you can fill and that at some point someone will notice. That's what the voice hides. Not productivity. Not discipline. Not work ethic. Fear dressed up as high standards. And it's been charging me for the costume for years.

When I saw fear's face, I told it no. Not heroically. Tiredly. The way you say no to something after years of saying yes without thinking. I told it: you've done enough. Not enough forever. Not enough to retire. Enough for today. For this Tuesday. For this moment when the computer is all green and there's no stone to set. Enough, man. Sit still for a while.

There's a difference between being enough and feeling enough. The first is a fact. It's measured in what exists, in teams that run on their own, in projects that move forward, in cathedrals still standing. The second is a decision. You choose it. You practice it. You fight for it every day against the voice that says you should be doing more, being more, filling more. And anxiety feeds on the distance between the two. On the gap between what you are and what you feel you should be. That gap never fully closes. There will always be a bigger pair of pants waiting. There will always be another level, another cathedral. And the shadow will grow with each one.

But the jungle already showed me something, even if it took me weeks to understand it: silence isn't emptiness. It's space. And space doesn't need filling. It needs living in. The howler monkeys aren't afraid of the dawn. They announce it. The jungle doesn't need anyone to be deafening. And you (and I), man, don't need to be in constant motion to deserve the pants you put on. Sometimes the value is in what you've already done. In the cathedral still standing while the builder has a coffee and watches the mist without thinking about anything.

Whatever's left gets done tomorrow. In the same pants. That will keep feeling too big. And that will keep being mine.
