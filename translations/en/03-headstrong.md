# Headstrong

*On subcultures, genuine obsessions, and the difference between getting absorbed in something and building yourself from the inside*

> *"Life hack: stop trying to be cool. Be nerdy and obsessive about the things you love. Enthusiasm will get you farther than indifference."*

---

I was twelve or thirteen the first time I read about the Dunning-Kruger effect. Not in class, not because anyone recommended it: I found it on my own, in a book, the way I found most things back then. I was the kind of kid who'd rather stay reading during First Communion rehearsals while everyone else did something more reasonable. The kind who, to this day, would rather watch a documentary than a movie. I could count my friends on one hand and still have fingers left over.

I bring it up because that picture, a kid obsessing over a cognitive bias while everyone his age is doing literally anything else, isn't filler. It's the earliest record I have of something that still defines me: not wanting to half-understand things. Staying with a subject until I've exhausted it, and then a little longer. Not settling for knowing that something exists when I could understand how it works.

There's something about that impulse I want to name before going on. It isn't about piling up information for the fun of it. It isn't intellectual collecting or vanity in disguise. It's something more basic: a deep discomfort with shallowness. A kind of vertigo that yells at you, accuses you of disrespecting the subject, if for one second you think about stopping before you really get it. That feeling, which at twelve made me look weird and left me friendless at parties, is exactly the same one that today has me measuring water temperature before I make a coffee, or dancing salsa until my body begs for mercy, or walking into industries I know nothing about, certain I'll understand them if I give them enough time.

For years I thought it was a quirk of my wiring I had to manage quietly. Now I understand it was the first brick of an identity. And the difference between a borrowed identity and a built one is exactly there: whether the bricks came from outside, or whether you fired them yourself, slowly, obsessively, in the low heat of genuine curiosity.

---

## I. What your surroundings do to your self

We live surrounded by subcultures. Some we choose consciously; others just find us: the music we started listening to at fifteen because it was playing in our older brother's room, the aesthetic we adopted when we wanted to belong to something bigger than ourselves, the beliefs we absorbed from our surroundings before we'd examined them with the care they deserved. The problem isn't that our environment shapes us. That's inevitable and, in many ways, a gift. The problem is when we stop noticing it's happening. When the absorption is so complete that you can no longer tell what you chose from what simply rubbed off on you.

I think about this every time I remember *I'm No Longer Here* (*Ya no estoy aquí*), Fernando Frías's film. There's something in it that captures this dynamic with an honesty that's uncomfortable precisely because it doesn't judge. Its characters use music and dance as the only thread of light in a closed, shadowed world with no apparent exit. The long, tense shots aren't an arbitrary style choice: they're the visual weight of an identity that lives on the margins, that depends entirely on a subculture to hold itself up because outside there's nothing else to hold it. Ulises doesn't dance because he likes dancing. He dances because dancing is all he has. Slowed-down cumbia isn't a genre to him; it's the only language in which his existence makes sense.

What works as tragedy in the film is also a mirror of a process more common than it looks. The logic of dissolving into something that gives you belonging before you're clear on who you are is a permanent temptation, and you don't need to live on the edges of Monterrey to fall for it. It's in the clothes you adopt to fit in without asking whether you actually like them. It's in the opinions you repeat because they're the group's, the ones that get likes, the ones that avoid conflict. It's in beliefs we hold not because we've tested them but because they came wrapped in community identity, so questioning the belief feels like betraying the people who share it.

There's a psychological mechanism here worth understanding. Belonging is a basic need. It isn't vanity or weakness: it's evolutionary architecture. For most of human history, being cast out of the group meant death, and that wiring is still live even though the consequences have changed. The problem is that the same mechanism that protected us from lethal loneliness can now hijack identity if nobody's watching. You end up as the spokesperson for ideas you never examined, living an aesthetic you never fully chose, belonging to a subculture that defines you more than you define it. And the most insidious part is that it feels good. It feels like safety. It feels like knowing who you are, when it's actually the exact opposite.

Your surroundings will always leave a mark. There's no way to grow up in a vacuum, and it wouldn't be desirable even if it were possible. The question isn't whether they'll shape you, but how much of that process will be yours. How much you'll observe from a distance before adopting. How much you'll question before repeating. How much you'll choose consciously, knowing you could choose something else, instead of just drifting with whatever current you happened to land in.

## II. Being headstrong

There's a phrase in Colombia that's almost always used as a criticism: *"muy llevado de su parecer."* Roughly: headstrong, set on his own opinion. It's said about people who don't give in easily, who hold their opinions even when the group is pushing the other way, who'd rather be wrong on their own terms than right by following instructions. People have said it to me many times, and over the years I've learned to take it as a compliment in disguise.

I remember the exact moment I decided to stop taking notes in class. I was in eighth grade, fourteen years old, and I concluded that copying what the teacher wrote on the board was slowing me down. It wasn't teenage rebellion or laziness. It was an honest observation: when I copied, my attention split between the mechanical act of writing and the work of understanding, and neither came out well. I decided I'd rather listen with all the attention I had and trust that what mattered would stick. That decision, which my teachers found somewhere between worrying and arrogant, stayed with me through most of university. The point wasn't the study technique. It was the process: observe, question, test, adjust based on results. The difference between adopting a practice because it works for you and adopting it because it's what everyone does.

The same process has repeated itself in bigger decisions. Starting a company in my third semester of university, when you're supposedly meant to be focused on passing your classes. Getting into oncology for a deeply personal reason, wanting to understand what was happening to my grandmother, wanting to contribute something from what I know how to do, and discovering that the initial motivation was turning into something more permanent. Going into crypto when the territory was new and confusing, raising capital, launching in Dubai, learning at speed everything I didn't know about cash flow, leading teams, and negotiating under pressure. Going to work in Germany and getting one of those lessons you only learn when things go very wrong: the one I wrote about in the first piece of this series, the one where I got robbed and that turned into an offshore master's degree in resilience.

None of those decisions came from following a manual or copying what other people were doing. They came from reading the terrain, forming an opinion, acting on it, and adjusting along the way. Sometimes it worked. Sometimes it didn't. But either way, the learning was mine, not borrowed.

> *"Comparison is the thief of joy."* — Theodore Roosevelt

Comparing yourself to others can be useful, but not as a source of envy or a measure of success. It's useful as a sideways reference: what are they doing that I could learn from? What mistakes are they making that I could avoid? But when comparison becomes the thermometer of your own worth, it robs you of the ability to enjoy the process. The comparison that really matters is always with yourself. Am I better than yesterday? Do I know more than last month? Are the decisions I'm making taking me where I want to go? That's the only metric that doesn't depend on variables I don't control.

## III. The pillars nobody assigned me

I've been at this coffee thing for eight years, and it's only gotten worse over time. In the good way.

I started because my parents always drank good coffee. Not supermarket coffee, not *tinto de olla*, the pot-brewed coffee most Colombians know as "coffee": that watery, bitter drink left boiling for hours that has about as much to do with specialty coffee as boxed wine has to do with a Burgundy. My parents looked for coffee from small producers. They bought it at fairs, at specialty shops, in those corners where the people who know meet the people who grow. And something from those cups stayed with me. But I could never recreate them at home. There was always a variable I didn't control, something lost between the origin and the mug. And that inconsistency turned into a pursuit.

What strikes me now, looking back, is that the pursuit follows exactly the same logic as reading about Dunning-Kruger at twelve, or deciding to stop taking notes in eighth grade. I didn't want to make good coffee. I wanted to understand coffee. And really understanding it meant going all the way down the rabbit hole: bean variety, growing altitude, fermentation processes, roast, grind, water temperature, extraction time, the minerals in the water you're extracting with, the pour pattern, the pour speed, the geometry of the brewer. Variables most people find excessive and that, to me, are just routine and habit.

Colombia produces some of the most complex coffees in the world, and most Colombians drink it watered down without a second thought about what's behind the cup. That isn't a criticism: it's the result of decades of an industry that exported the best and left the worst for the domestic market. Giving coffee the attention it deserves isn't a snobby hobby. It's a form of respect for a deeply Colombian product that deserves to be understood seriously.

I know how all this sounds. I know that when someone starts talking about extraction temperature and water minerals, the knee-jerk reaction is to slap a "snob" label on them and keep walking. It's worth taking that apart, because there is a kind of snob who deserves contempt: the guy with no identity of his own who builds one by force, for show, picking tastes the way you'd pick a costume. That snob doesn't want to understand anything. He wants to be seen understanding. But there's something else that gets mistaken for snobbery and is really curiosity followed all the way through. There's a name for that automatic pushback against people who want to know more: anti-intellectualism. The idea that going deep on something is pretentious, that being able to tell a Gesha from a Caturra makes you unbearable, that preferring a Coffea coffee to a supermarket brand isn't a legitimate taste but a declaration of superiority.

I am a snob, if the definition is someone who cares about those differences. I'll own it. But my snobbery isn't a performance: I genuinely want to know why things taste the way they do, and then I like sharing it with whoever wants to listen. The difference is which way you're looking. The costume snob looks outward, at whoever's watching. I look inward, into the stuff itself.

The same obsession is where my interest in wine came from: another system where terroir matters, where human decisions transform the raw material, where a glass is the summary of a thousand interacting variables. I'm not interested in wine as a collection of labels or a way to look sophisticated. I'm interested in the same question that hooks me about coffee: why does this taste like this and not something else? What decisions made it possible? That curiosity about the mechanism behind the result is the thread connecting interests that look scattered from the outside.

## IV. The rhythm that came first

I didn't choose salsa. Salsa chose me, long before I was capable of choosing anything. My mom says that when I was just a few months old and couldn't sleep, the fix was to put on Grupo Niche and dance with me in the living room. The rhythm calmed me when nothing else worked. Before I could walk, my body was already answering those timbales, those trumpets, that clave that holds everything together without you noticing until you go looking for it.

I didn't grow up in a dance academy. I didn't take formal lessons until much later. My bond with salsa didn't come from training or from a conscious decision to learn a genre. It came from something more primitive: a body that recognizes a rhythm as its own and moves before the mind decides whether it should.

Today salsa is one of the few things that gets me completely out of my head. Coffee is active meditation, but it's still a mental process: there are variables to monitor, decisions to make, adjustments to dial in. Salsa is something else. Salsa is surrendering to the rhythm, letting the body do what it knows how to do, trusting an intelligence that doesn't pass through conscious thought.

It's also a deeply beautiful genre. I don't say that out of nostalgia or the easy patriotism of "ours is the best." I say it because salsa, done right, has a complexity that rewards attention. The layers of instrumentation, the way the piano and bass talk to each other, the tension between clave and melody, horn arrangements that can be aggressive or melancholy or euphoric depending on what the song needs. There's a sophistication there that doesn't always get its due, maybe because the genre is so tied to partying that people forget the craft behind it.

Salsa came to me from my surroundings. But the decision to keep dancing it, to seek it out, to prefer it when I could be listening to anything else, that decision is mine. And that distinction, between what your environment puts in front of you and what you decide to cultivate, is exactly what this piece is about.

## V. The problem with getting absorbed

There's an important difference between letting something shape you and letting something define you completely. The first is natural, almost inevitable, and often desirable. The second is a quiet abdication.

I keep coming back to *I'm No Longer Here* because the film shows this difference with painful clarity. Ulises isn't just part of the Los Terkos subculture. Ulises *is* Los Terkos. Outside that group, outside that music, outside that dance, there's no recognizable Ulises. When circumstances rip him out of that context and he ends up in New York, without his tribe, without the language of sound that gave his life meaning, what's left isn't a person adapting to a new place. What's left is a ghost. An identity that can't survive without the uniform.

When a subculture, a community, an aesthetic, or an ideology becomes the center of gravity of an identity, the person stops being the author of their own story and becomes a character in the group's narrative. Their tastes get standardized. Their opinions line up. The way they dress, talk, and relate to the world starts answering more to the group's codes than to anything they could trace back inside themselves.

And the most insidious part of that process is that it feels like identity. It feels like knowing who you are. Because in a way it is, just not your own. It's a rented identity that works as long as the belonging contract is in force, and falls apart when the group changes direction, kicks you out, or simply stops existing.

The mechanism has another face that's just as revealing: a group defines itself not only by what it is, but by what it isn't. Every collective needs its antagonist, the figure of "the other" that works as an inverted mirror and reinforces the belonging of everyone inside. You can see it clearly in cults, in that us-versus-them mentality, but it works exactly the same way in far more ordinary social groups. And what's especially brutal is that any hint that you resemble "the other," any gesture, opinion, or taste that crosses into enemy territory, is enough for the group to throw you out and strip you of the identity that was supposedly yours. Which only proves it never was.

That doesn't mean subcultures are bad or that belonging to something is a mistake. Communities can be where you discover parts of yourself you'd never have found alone. The problem isn't belonging; it's dependence. It's when you need the group to know who you are, when there's nothing recognizable left outside the tribe, when the identity can't survive without the uniform.

The question I find useful is this: what of what I believe, wear, listen to, defend, and practice would still be mine if nobody were watching? If there were no group to validate and no image to keep up? If the community that gave me this language disappeared tomorrow, would I still speak it? Whatever's left after that question is what's really yours. The rest is noise your surroundings left stuck to you.

## VI. Bare brick doesn't mean malleable

In the first piece of this series I wrote about the architecture of accumulation: the idea that you don't delete anything, you archive it, and you build on top of everything that's already there. That being in *obra negra*, bare brick, structurally up but unfinished, is the natural state of someone who decides not to stop growing.

But bare brick doesn't mean an empty lot. A building in *obra negra* already has its foundations poured, its columns up, its structural skeleton defined. It isn't a vacant lot where any passing wind can put up whatever it likes. The fact that you're in progress, that the finishes are missing, that some spaces are still undefined, doesn't mean you're infinitely moldable or that any outside influence can redefine you from scratch.

I've met people who confuse being open to growth with having no judgment. Who read flexibility as an invitation to go with whatever current comes by next. Who use the language of process and construction as an excuse not to take positions, not to defend anything, not to be anyone in particular while they wait for their surroundings to tell them who they should be. That isn't bare brick. That's an empty lot with a "For Rent" sign.

Having character isn't the opposite of being under construction. It's the requirement for the construction to make sense. A building that doesn't know what it wants to be ends up as the sum of every architectural fashion that passed by while it was going up: a Brutalist floor, an Art Deco floor, a minimalist floor, no internal coherence, no recognizable identity. What saves it isn't finishing, because the work never finishes, but being clear about the structural principles that define it from the inside.

What are those principles? They vary from person to person, but the category is recognizable. It's the ethical code you don't negotiate, the lines you won't cross even when crossing them would be convenient. It's how you relate to knowledge: whether you settle for the surface or need to go deeper. It's the set of opinions you've formed over time with real information, not social pressure or the wish to fit in. It's what you defend when defending it is uncomfortable, what you hold onto when the group pushes you to change.

Those principles aren't dogmas. They're columns. A column is structural; a dogma is defensive.

Being bare brick, then, isn't not knowing who you are. It's knowing who you are so far, having columns while you keep adding floors, accepting that the blueprint is subject to revision without that meaning there's no blueprint.

## VII. The generalist and the honesty of Dunning-Kruger

When I read about the Dunning-Kruger effect at twelve, what stuck with me wasn't the description of the phenomenon itself, the idea that incompetent people overestimate their abilities while competent people underestimate theirs, but how uncomfortable it is to recognize it in yourself.

There's a curve people use to illustrate it. It starts with a peak of unjustified confidence, "Mount Stupid," where you know enough to think you know a lot but not enough to notice what you're missing. Then comes the drop: the Valley of Despair, where you start to see the real size of the territory and your confidence collapses. And then, slowly, a gradual climb, the Plateau of Sustainability, where real competence starts to build on the humility of knowing you'll never fully master the field.

What hit me at twelve was understanding that you don't cross the curve just once. Every time you get into something new, every time you explore a discipline you didn't know, you go back to the start. You're back on Mount Stupid, even though you know it exists. And the only difference between someone who's crossed the curve many times and someone crossing it for the first time is that the first one recognizes the terrain. They know the early confidence is fake, that the despair in the valley is temporary, that real competence is on the other side of a long process with no shortcuts.

That early reading left a concrete mark on how I relate to the things I care about. With coffee, I didn't want to be the guy who makes good coffee; I wanted to understand coffee, knowing that really understanding it would take years. When I got into oncology, I didn't want to be someone who works in the sector; I wanted to understand the whole system, the friction, the possibilities. When I got into crypto, knowing how to use the tools wasn't enough; I wanted to understand the foundations that made them possible.

Over time, that impulse led me somewhere I took a while to name: generalism. David Epstein describes it in *Range* with a clarity that felt familiar. In a world that celebrates early specialization and rewards deep mastery of a single field, generalists seem out of place. They take longer to find their path. They move between disciplines with a discomfort specialists don't have. They look scattered, indecisive, unable to commit.

But that same range lets them see connections specialists don't. It lets them solve problems that don't fit in any box, carry lessons from one field into another, and adapt to contexts that change faster than any specialty can keep up with. In a predictable world, the specialist wins. In a complex, uncertain world, which is the one we live in, the generalist has advantages that aren't obvious until you need them.

For me, being a generalist means something concrete: I can walk into new territory and build reasonable competence in a reasonable amount of time, not because I have some special talent, but because I've crossed the curve enough times to know how it's crossed. I know the early confusion is normal. I know the feeling of knowing nothing is temporary. I know the process has predictable stages and that patience is more useful than panic.

That doesn't replace deep specialization when it's needed. But it lets you connect specialties, translate between disciplines, and see the whole system when each specialist only sees their piece. It's a different role, not a better or worse one. And it's the role that fits best with how I relate to knowledge.

## VIII. Building in community

I've talked about being headstrong, about forming my own opinions, about not dissolving into the group. But none of that means building alone.

> *"If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."* — African proverb

What I've learned over years of starting companies, leading teams, and working in unfamiliar territory is that building alone has a low ceiling. Your own ideas need to rub against other ideas to get sharp. Opinions that never get debated turn brittle, however solid they look. Honest feedback, given and received, is the material that lets you correct course before mistakes get expensive.

I've always tried to build with a logic that seems healthy to me: share what I know without holding anything back, ask for what I need without embarrassment, give feedback with the same honesty I'd want to receive it. I've seen that this way of operating gets better results for everyone, me included. Knowledge you share comes back multiplied. Relationships built on honesty carry more weight than ones built on empty politeness.

Being headstrong doesn't mean ignoring what other people think. It means forming your own view before comparing it, holding it when it makes sense, and adjusting it when the evidence says otherwise. It means listening to understand, not just to respond. It means actively seeking out people who think differently, because those are the people who can teach you the most.

Identity is built from the inside, yes. But it isn't built in isolation. It's built in conversation with the world, taking what's useful, discarding what isn't, and always staying clear on who's the architect and who's just visiting.

## IX. The foundations are your own

When I look back, almost everything I am has an outside origin. Coffee came from my parents. Salsa came from my mom. My interest in oncology started with my grandmother. The drive to understand things was sparked by a book I found on my own, but that someone wrote and someone published and someone put somewhere I could find it. Nothing comes from nothing.

Your surroundings will always plant things. They'll drop seeds in your path, some you asked for and many you didn't. The question is who waters, who prunes, who decides which way the growth points. You can let your surroundings plant and water and prune too, and end up as a garden designed by other people. Or you can take the seeds that interest you, discard the ones that don't, and take charge of the growing even if you didn't choose the soil.

My mom gave me salsa. But she didn't make me keep dancing it twenty-something years later. My parents showed me coffee could be something else. But they didn't push me down the rabbit hole of fermentation and roasting. My grandmother's cancer brought me to oncology. But the decision to stay, to turn that first exposure into something lasting, was mine.

Being bare brick isn't an excuse for vagueness. It isn't saying "I still don't know who I am" as a way to dodge the commitment of being someone. It's a commitment to the process of defining yourself without rushing, without giving in to the pressure of having it all figured out ahead of time, but also without giving up the responsibility of figuring it out as you go. Bare brick has structure. It has columns. It has a blueprint, even if that blueprint is always up for revision.

Your surroundings will shape you. That's inevitable. But identity, the kind that actually carries the weight of a life, the kind that survives when the group changes its code or its aesthetic or its ideology, the kind that's still recognizable when the uniform wears thin and the tribe scatters, can only come from the inside.
