# Talk Is Cheap

*On the real price of knowing something, the experts who aren't, and why I'd rather be wrong out loud*

When *Project Hail Mary* came out, I devoured it in two days. I'd been following Andy Weir since *The Martian*, and something about the premise pulled at me with an urgency I couldn't rationalize: a guy wakes up alone on a spaceship with no memories and has to save humanity without knowing exactly how. He isn't the best-prepared astronaut. He isn't the most brilliant scientist of his generation. He isn't the hero the mission needed. He's a middle-school science teacher with one quality that, as the novel goes on, turns out to be worth more than all the specialization in the world: the ability to admit what he doesn't know and build from there.

Grace survives because he doesn't fake it. When something doesn't add up, he doesn't improvise an explanation to save face. He sits down, observes, tests, gets it wrong, adjusts. And when he meets Rocky, a being who doesn't speak his language, doesn't share his biology, and has zero interest in impressing him, together they build something neither could have built alone. Not from expertise. From the radical honesty of two beings who know they don't know, and who decide to work with that instead of against it.

---

### I. The opinion economy

We're living in the moment in history when more people give opinions about more things with fewer consequences than ever. The platforms we use to communicate are designed, down to their most basic architecture, to reward opinion over observation. The fast take wins. Nuance loses. Whoever says "I don't know" vanishes from the feed. Whoever says "this is how it is, period" gets the engagement.

What interests me isn't that people give a lot of opinions. It's the motive behind them. There's the person who gives an opinion because they thought about it; they exist, but they're the minority. There's the one who gives an opinion to sell, who packages their position as knowledge when it's really a pitch in disguise. There's the one who gives an opinion to stir things up, who figured out that outrage is the cheapest engagement on the market. And there's the one who gives an opinion because silence is punished with invisibility, who posts not because they have something to say but because the algorithm penalizes whoever says nothing.

Fake gurus are the most grotesque version of this ecosystem, but not the most dangerous. The guy selling a course on how to make money without ever having made money from anything except selling courses. The perfect epistemological pyramid scheme. At least that one is easy to spot. The subtle version does more damage: the person preaching the latest buzzword with an arrogance that can't survive three questions of depth. The one who says "artificial intelligence" in every sentence but can't explain what a transformer is. Technically, they're not lying. They're telling a version of reality so heavily made up that it works like a lie without being one. And that's worse, because you can't accuse someone of lying when what they're doing is omitting, exaggerating, and packaging. But the result is the same: the person on the other side walks away believing they know something they don't.

Nassim Taleb diagnosed the core mechanism in *Skin in the Game*: the problem isn't that people have opinions. It's that they have them with nothing at stake. The analyst recommending stocks he'd never buy. The consultant designing strategies she'd never execute. When the distance between your opinion and its consequences is infinite, the opinion gets cheaper until it's worth nothing. And the most insidious part is that the cheap opinion, in a feed, on a podcast, on a conference stage, looks exactly like the expensive one. The packaging is identical. The difference only shows when you scratch the surface, and almost nobody scratches, because the next take is already waiting.

### II. What it costs to know something

I used to have a very strong opinion about how many hours you had to work to be successful. More hours, more sacrifice, more hustle, better results. It was a clean equation, easy to defend. The kind of opinion that looks great in a LinkedIn post with fire emojis.

I worked thirty-hour days. Eighteen on average, for years. I slept in offices. I stopped eating for weeks. I lived on chocolate milk and cigarettes. And what I learned wasn't that extreme sacrifice produces extreme results. What I learned was that sacrifice without structure produces destruction. That hours mean nothing if what you're building has no foundation. That "how much do you work" is the favorite metric of people who don't have better ones.

Today my opinion about work is radically different. I don't believe in hustle as a philosophy. I believe in intensity applied intelligently, in being able to tell the difference between being busy and being productive, and above all, I believe the goal should be to live. That work is a tool for building the life you want, not a substitute for it. That the romanticizing of burnout is a scam that benefits the people who preach it and destroys the people who practice it.

I didn't read that opinion anywhere. I built it with the most expensive material there is: the experience of having been wrong. And that's what gives it weight. Not that it's right, but that it has a cost behind it. Every part of that opinion has a scar attached. And when an opinion has cost you like that, you don't drop it because of social pressure, but you do drop it because of evidence. Because you already know what being wrong costs, and you'd rather pay the price of changing your mind than the price of clinging to one that's already been proven false.

That's what *strong opinions, loosely held* means. Start with strong opinions, formed with the best you have at the time, and let arguments and experience reshape them. Don't cling to your original take as if it were your identity. Understand that the strongest opinion you can have is the one that's already survived several deaths and several resurrections, because each time it fell and got back up, it got up in better shape.

The problem with hot takes isn't that they exist. It's that people defend them as dogma. The guy who's never changed his mind isn't steadfast. He's the one who's paid the least attention to reality. And the people who pay reality the least attention are almost always the ones who paid the least for what they believe. Because when the opinion is free, defending it to the death is free too. Nothing's at stake. No scar to remind you what being wrong feels like. Just a take, a microphone, and an audience that applauds certainty without asking where it came from.

### III. I don't consider myself an expert in anything

After everything I've done, the last thing I'd do is call myself an expert in anything.

That isn't a humble pose. It's the logical consequence of what I described in the second piece of this series about the Dunning-Kruger curve: the more times you cross it, the more respect you have for the ground still left to cover. I won't repeat the explanation here (anyone who wants to can go read it), but the gist is this: the word "expert" should make you suspicious. Because whoever declares themselves an expert is implicitly saying they've arrived. That the process is over. And in my experience, that declaration correlates far more with needing to sell something than with actual knowledge.

I've met incredibly specialized people. People who've spent decades immersed in a single field. What strikes me most about them, the truly competent ones, is that none of them calls themselves an expert. They cite other people's views to build their argument. They openly acknowledge the limits of what they know. They work as aggregators of knowledge: they gather, process, connect, synthesize, question. But they don't plant themselves as the final authority. Real depth produces humility. Shallowness produces certainty.

Richard Feynman said that if you can't explain something simply, you don't really understand it. Applied rigorously, that test eliminates ninety percent of people who present themselves as experts. Because unnecessary complexity isn't a sign of depth. It's a hiding place. It's jargon used as a barrier to entry so nobody can question what's being said. Real knowledge simplifies. Ignorance in disguise complicates.

When I don't know something, I say so. Directly. But there's a nuance that matters: saying "I don't know" isn't the same as saying "I can't." When I say "I don't know this yet," the key word is *yet*. What I'm communicating isn't insecurity. It's confidence in something else: that I have a proven method for getting from not knowing to knowing enough. Confidence in the process, not the answer. Confidence that the problem can be solved if you ask the right questions, find the right information, and iterate honestly. It isn't an "I don't know" that paralyzes. It's an "I don't know" that comes with a route already planned.

Some clients don't know what to do with that transparency. They expect packaged certainty and get a map with areas marked blank. Some prefer the person who shows up with a flawless PowerPoint and answers ready to go. But upfront certainty, when it's genuine, is incredibly rare. And when it isn't genuine, it's dangerous. Because what's being bought isn't knowledge; it's the feeling of knowledge. And that feeling falls apart on first contact with reality.

In *Conclave*, the character who thinks most clearly is the one with the least agenda. When your opinion has no sale price, when you're not trying to close something or impress anyone, you can see the problem as it is. No distortion. No filter of what it's convenient to say. That clarity, which looks like a luxury, should be the bare minimum for anyone who wants to have an opinion on something that matters.

### IV. The feedback you didn't ask for

There's a kind of feedback that has no format, no teaching intention, that nobody designed so you'd take it well. It doesn't arrive in a feedback meeting or a quarterly form or wrapped in the praise-criticism-praise sandwich everyone knows and nobody respects. It arrives without asking permission. Sometimes from reality itself, naked and indifferent. Sometimes from a person who said what they saw without calculating whether you were ready to hear it. Sometimes from a circumstance that wasn't in any plan. And it is, by far, the most valuable kind there is. Not because it's the most pleasant (it almost never is) but because it's the least contaminated. It wasn't calibrated to tell you what you want to hear. It didn't go through the "how will he react?" filter before reaching your door.

Think about it for a second. When you ask for feedback, what you get is already edited. The person giving it is calculating, consciously or not, what they can say without damaging the relationship, without starting a conflict, without putting you on the defensive. They're selecting. They're softening. They're choosing their words with the care of someone who knows you asked and therefore expect something you can digest. That's not useless, but it has a ceiling. Requested feedback rarely contains the information you most need, because the information you most need is almost always what nobody would dare give you if you asked.

In Germany, in the middle of what I told in another piece of this series (please read it if you haven't), a friend told me my voice sounded different. That what he was seeing on the other side of the screen wasn't me. He didn't say it to hurt me. He wasn't evaluating my performance. He didn't have a framework. He said what he saw, unfiltered, without checking first, without asking whether I was ready to hear it. And that sentence, said with no ceremony, was more useful than any formal intervention I could have gotten, because it contained the truth I couldn't see from inside. If I'd asked him "how do I seem to you?" he'd probably have softened it. He'd probably have said something about stress, about how it's normal at a startup, something that let me keep functioning without facing what was happening. But I didn't ask. And because I didn't ask, what he gave me was the unedited version. The version that hurt. The version that helped.

Recently, a bot we built negotiated a contract at a price ten times lower than it should have. Controls were missing, guardrails were missing, things were missing that we should have put in place before letting an automated system make decisions with real consequences. There was no mentor who sat down with me to break down the mistake. There was a number on a screen. And that number taught me more about the importance of controls in AI systems than any paper or conference. Reality doesn't call a meeting to give you notes. It sends you the result and leaves you alone with it.

I love screwing up. I say that because it's literally true: every screw-up comes with a lesson you can't get any other way. Theory tells you what can go wrong. A screw-up tells you what it feels like when it does, how much it costs, how long it takes to fix, who it affects, which part of your process had a hole you hadn't seen. Theoretical knowledge informs. A mistake you've lived through transforms. And the difference between the two isn't one of degree. It's one of kind. You can read a hundred articles on guardrails in automated systems and nod along to every one. Or you can lose real money for not having them, and I guarantee that lesson installs itself in a part of your brain no article can reach.

In *Jiro Dreams of Sushi*, Jiro Ono hands the plate back to the apprentice without telling him what was wrong. The apprentice has to figure it out alone. Make it two hundred times, five hundred, however many it takes until his body understands what verbal instruction can't convey. It's a brutal method. And it produces masters. Not because brutality is good teaching, but because it forces the apprentice to develop something no explanation can give: the ability to read reality for himself. To look at the result, compare it with the intention, spot the gap, and correct it. Without anyone pointing the way. That isn't information you store. It's judgment you form.

But there's something I want to make clear, because the conversation about feedback has been distorted in a dangerous way. When people think of "honest feedback," the image that comes up is the boss who tears you apart in a meeting, or the guy who says something hurtful and then justifies it with "I'm just direct." That version of honesty is laziness dressed up as courage. Real honesty takes something much harder than saying the first thing that comes to mind: it takes saying what matters in a way the other person can use.

Well-framed honest feedback isn't an attack. It's a signal. It's someone telling you "this isn't working" with the implicit confidence that you can fix it. It's a partner telling you "you're making a decision with incomplete information" before it's too late. It's a friend telling you your voice sounds different, that something changed, that what they're seeing doesn't match the person they know. It doesn't ask permission. It doesn't wait for the perfect moment. It arrives when it arrives. And its value lies precisely in not having been requested, because whatever's requested already comes filtered through what the other person thinks you want to hear.

The problem is that giving that kind of feedback takes effort. It takes knowing the person, calibrating what information they need, thinking about how to say it in a way that lights things up instead of wrecking them. And because it takes effort, most people take one of two shortcuts: either they say everything unfiltered and blame the other person if they can't digest it, or they say nothing and let the problem grow in silence.

Both shortcuts are cowardly. The first dresses up as courage. The second dresses up as kindness.

And of the two, the second is more dangerous. Badly framed feedback can be corrected, clarified, tried again in better form. Feedback that was never given becomes a problem that grows in the dark until it's too late. I've seen teams collapse not from a lack of talent but from an excess of politeness. Brilliant people who saw the problems clearly and decided to say nothing because the environment didn't feel "safe" for honesty. The problem wasn't that the environment was dangerous. The problem was that they'd confused safety with the absence of friction. And the absence of friction isn't a sign of health. It's a sign that something stopped moving.

Ray Dalio built Bridgewater's culture around what he called *radical transparency*: an environment where anyone, regardless of rank, can and should point out what isn't working. It's a model with real caveats: not everyone is ready to operate that way, not every context allows it, and taken to the extreme it can create environments that confuse transparency with aggression. But the underlying philosophy seems hard to argue with, and it's one I try to apply in what I do: the unfiltered truth, framed intelligently and respectfully, is always more useful than the kind lie. Always.

What I see, and it worries me more and more, is that people spend more energy building walls against information they don't want to hear than developing the ability to process it.

And the walls aren't obvious. Nobody says "I don't want to know the truth." The walls are subtler than that. They're the filters we set up around ourselves so that only information confirming what we already believe gets in. The curated feed where everyone thinks alike. The social circle where disagreement reads as aggression. The way we choose who to listen to: not by the quality of what they say, but by how comfortable it makes us feel. Each of those decisions, taken alone, seems reasonable. Taken together, they build a sealed chamber where nothing new can get in.

And what you lose inside that chamber isn't comfort. There's plenty of comfort. What you lose is the ability to grow. Because growth, by definition, requires new information. And new information, by definition, is uncomfortable. There's no way to learn something you didn't know without going through the moment of realizing you didn't know it. That moment is uncomfortable. Always. Whether it comes from a data point, a person, a failure, or a hard conversation. Discomfort is the signal that something is coming in that wasn't there before. And if the environment you operate in is designed so that signal never fires, what you have isn't a safe space. It's a stagnation pod with nice lighting.

There's a huge difference between protecting someone from harm and protecting them from information. The first is care. The second is sabotage dressed as care. Harm should be avoided. Information, even when it hurts, should be let through. Because the alternative, a world where people only hear what they want to hear, only read what confirms what they already think, only surround themselves with voices that reflect their own image back without distortion, is a world where nobody finds out they're wrong until it's too late to fix.

And that's exactly what I see happening. People who haven't changed their minds about anything substantial in years and who read that rigidity as firmness. People who confuse the absence of confrontation with the presence of peace. People who get more comfortable every year and don't realize that growing comfort is the clearest symptom that they've stopped exposing themselves to what they don't control. The information you don't want to hear doesn't disappear because you shut the door on it. It's still out there, piling up, waiting for the moment the door can't hold. And when that moment comes, and it always does, the blow is proportional to how long you went without listening.

### V. The archive you don't edit

In the earlier pieces of this series I've talked about archiving instead of deleting. About the recycle bin as a place I barely know. About bare brick as a natural state. If you haven't read them, they're on my profile and give the full context for what I'm about to say.

What I want to add, what this piece forced me to think about in a way the earlier ones hadn't, is that there's one category of archive that matters more than the rest: the record of the times I was wrong.

Not the wins. Not the moments when everything clicked and the result confirmed what I already believed. Those files are pleasant but easy. The ones that hold up the structure, the ones that really determine the quality of the decisions I make today, are the records of error. The opinion I had to let go of. The feedback that showed up uninvited and held the truth I couldn't see. The certainty that crumbled. The project that revealed exactly where the holes in my process were.

Those files don't get edited. They don't get touched up in hindsight. They don't get turned into "learning experiences" with a pretty bow on top. They're kept exactly as they were: uncomfortable, raw, sometimes embarrassing. Because their value isn't in the story you can build around them. Their value is in the information they contain. And that information, the kind you only get by being wrong, is the material you calibrate judgment with. Every opinion I hold today is better than the previous version not because I'm smarter, but because the archive of mistakes is bigger. And a bigger archive of mistakes means a finer filter for the decisions ahead.

That's why I don't call myself an expert. Because whoever calls themselves an expert is saying their archive of mistakes doesn't need new entries. And the day my archive stops growing is the day I stopped paying attention.

That's why I don't package my experience into tips or frameworks or exportable methodologies. Because packaging is compressing, and compressing means losing the nuances that make information useful. The nuances are in what went wrong, not in the polished summary of what went right.

That's why I don't sell certainties. Because the ones I had fell apart so many times that I learned to treat them as what they are: hypotheses with an expiration date.

And that's why I'm not afraid to say "I don't know." Because "I don't know" followed by "but I know how to find out" is the most honest and solid starting point I know. And honesty, in the end, is the only foundation you can build something on that won't collapse when reality sends you the bill.

Talk is cheap. And when something's cheap, it's worth asking why.

The most expensive opinions I have, the ones that cost me money, time, relationships, health, and a few that nearly cost me my sanity, are the ones I least dare to state with a tone of absolute certainty. Because I know what being wrong costs. Because I know the next correction could show up tomorrow, uninvited, unformatted, without the slightest intention of being kind. And when it does, I'll archive it the way I archive everything: unedited, untouched, and certain that this new data point will make the next opinion better than this one.

The guy who's never changed his mind isn't steadfast. He's the one who's paid the least attention to reality.
