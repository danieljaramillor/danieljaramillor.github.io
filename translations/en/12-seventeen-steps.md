# Seventeen Steps

*On a coffee ritual that's never the same, perception as a craft, and everything we climb without counting*

#### I. Automate that, boss

I make a living automating things. I've spent years building systems so that repetitive work gets done by a machine instead of a person: agents that answer calls, flows that move data from one place to another, processes that used to need someone sitting there for eight hours and now run on their own while that someone does something else with their life. My eye is trained for it. Wherever there's a task that repeats, I see an inefficiency waiting to be solved, a little rattle in the engine that needs fixing. It's an occupational hazard and I own it completely.

And every morning, before the day starts, I commit the same inconsistency: I spend ten minutes of slow, voluntary manual labor making a coffee a machine would make faster, with less error, and without me.

Those machines exist. I know them better than most, because knowing machines is my job. They measure temperature better than I do, repeat the recipe without getting tired, don't get distracted, don't have bad days. Any efficiency analysis I'd run on a client's process, applied to my own kitchen, comes to the same conclusion without a second thought: automate that, boss. And yet there I am every morning, weighing beans on a scale, grinding them, heating the water, wetting the filter, pouring in circles with a care nobody is asking of me. Ten minutes defended tooth and nail by a guy who gets paid to eliminate exactly that.

For years I thought the explanation was the obvious one: that I liked the ritual because it was always the same. An anchor. The same sequence of gestures to start the day on solid ground. It sounds nice and it's a lie. Because if there's one thing I've learned doing this every day, it's that the ritual is never the same. The beans change, because the bag runs out and another arrives from another farm, with another process, another roast, another date. The grind changes, because it has to be adjusted to the new beans. The ratio changes, the method changes if I feel like it, the water changes. Even the physics changes: in Bogotá water gives up and boils at around ninety-two degrees Celsius; in Cali it holds out until almost ninety-seven. Not even the boiling point, which you'd think is the most fixed thing in a kitchen, stays put for me from one city to the other. I live between two altitudes, and the same ritual doesn't obey the same laws in both.

So no, the constant isn't the recipe. There is no recipe. The only thing that doesn't change, the only thing that really repeats every morning, is that there's a ritual. The appointment. Everything else is variable. Think of it like grammar: the sentences never repeat, but the structure holding them up does. I haven't spent years making the same coffee. I've spent years showing up to the same appointment with a different coffee.

It started the way these things start, with a tiny act that didn't look like the door to anything: a scale. About eight years ago I weighed a dose of coffee for the first time. And notice that the word we hobbyists use is exactly that, *dose*, which is not an innocent word. From there it was a cheerful slide into the depths. First the scale, then the grinder, then the water, then the numbers. What I didn't know that day is that weighing the beans wasn't about becoming more demanding about coffee. It was something stranger: it was putting a frame around the act. And a frame, I learned later, isn't for making things come out the same. It's for being able to see how they differ.

### II. The seismograph

A fixed frame is what makes variation readable. Anyone who's set up an experiment knows it: to measure something, you have to hold everything else still. If everything moves at once, you can't see anything; it's pure noise. But if the structure doesn't move, if the appointment and the sequence and the way you stand in front of the act stay the same, then any difference becomes information. That's why those daily self-portrait projects work: the same face, the same framing, every day for years, and precisely because the framing never changes, you can watch the face age. The framing is the ritual. The photo is each day.

And what the coffee frame lets me see, most mornings, isn't the coffee. It's me.

On anxious days the pour comes out rushed. I don't notice it in my head; I notice it in the stream, which falls thicker and faster than it should, as if my hand were in a hurry to finish something that's barely started. On packed days, the kind where I wake up with the day's list already shouting at me, I don't even notice the bloom, that first wetting where the coffee swells, breathes, and releases its gas. And the bloom is the most beautiful part of the process. It happened right in front of me and I didn't see it. On good days, though, the water falls in a spiral as if it knew the way on its own, and there's a moment, just one, when the aroma rises and catches me off guard. The ritual doesn't calm me. That's another pretty lie I stopped telling myself. The ritual measures me. It's the instrument, not the medicine. A four-minute seismograph that runs every morning and tells me, before anyone else, what kind of day I'm wearing on the inside.

A Russian wrote about this more than a hundred years ago, and he got there by way of a sofa. Viktor Shklovsky tells how Tolstoy wrote down a tiny scene in his diary: he was cleaning his room and, when he got to the sofa, he couldn't remember whether he'd already dusted it or not. The gesture was so automatic it had done itself, without him. And the conclusion Tolstoy drew from that nothing, the one Shklovsky later turned into theory, is one of those lines that bites: if I did it without noticing, it's as if I hadn't done it. And if whole lives go by like that, eating without tasting, walking without seeing, loving on autopilot, then it's as if those lives never happened. Habit devours things, Shklovsky said. It devours furniture, clothes, your wife, and even the fear of war; that's how broad the man was. And art exists for the opposite: to de-automate perception, to make the stone stony again, so you go back to seeing what you now only recognize.

A coffee made on autopilot is Tolstoy's sofa. A cup nobody drank, even if the mug ends up empty. And for someone who's built a whole philosophy around archiving experiences, decisions, and lessons, that idea has a special edge, because the archive has a prerequisite almost nobody mentions: you can't archive what you didn't perceive. The morning that went by on autopilot isn't a blank page. It's worse: it's a page that never existed. Perception is the first archive. Everything else comes after, if it comes at all: the note, the photo, the essay.

### III. No undo button

Now, there's something about coffee that doesn't show up in any romantic description of the hobby: coffee has no undo button.

Water that's already poured doesn't go back in the kettle. A grind that came out too fine can't be made coarser. If the pour was too fast, if the temperature was off, if I ran over time, the cup that results is what it is, and there's no editing it. You can dump it and start over, sure, but that particular cup has already been written. In a world where my entire job has version control, where every document keeps a history and every system has its rollback, my mornings start with an act that doesn't forgive: four minutes of small, irreversible decisions, one after another, made with my hands.

And that law, that what's poured doesn't come back, has versions where what gets poured isn't water.

I know those versions. There's an animal that lives at the bottom of all of us and reacts in twelve milliseconds. That's how fast the old road in the brain sets off the alarm, before the reasoning part has finished climbing the stairs. Someone named it a hijacking, and the name fits. One night, at a party I'd arrived at with my partner, I saw something that left nothing to ask, and I felt the animal wake up with its fist already clenched, wanting the scene, the glass on the floor, the sentence you can't take back. Another time, far from home and in another language, I understood in a single afternoon that I'd been scammed, with money that wasn't only mine and people counting on me, and the animal wanted the opposite but just as irreversible: disappear, freeze, let go of the wheel. Two different blows, the same gap of milliseconds where the things that last forever get decided. On neither night did I give the animal what it wanted. I felt everything, and let that be clear: holding on isn't not feeling. But I didn't let it act.

For years I told those two nights as victories of cold blood, as if composure were a trait I'd come with from the factory. I don't tell them that way anymore. Because the real question isn't what I did those nights, but where the hand that knew how to wait came from. And the least epic, most honest answer I have is this: that hand had been training every morning for years, at a scale where getting it wrong didn't hurt. The method makes me, for example, wait for the bloom: thirty, forty seconds watching the coffee breathe, without intervening, with the hot water in my hand and the urge to keep going. Thirty seconds a day of doing nothing being exactly the thing to do. Where else do you train that? At what other point in the day do you practice, with your body and not with theory, the art of holding the impulse in your hand and not letting it go yet?

Nobody gets through the big night without having trained a thousand small mornings. Coffee is the gym. Life, every so often and without warning, sends the exam.

### IV. Two cups

The training has two sides, and two cups that look nothing alike taught them to me.

The first was an accident. One ordinary day I started a coffee and something interrupted me halfway through. I don't even remember what, which tells you it wasn't important. The coffee just sat there, open, half-poured, while I dealt with something else. I came back maybe half an hour later. The logical thing was to dump it: the water was lukewarm, the coffee had been exposed for half an hour, every manual says that cup is dead. I finished making it as it was, more so as not to waste the beans than out of faith. And it was exquisite. In capital letters, which is how I remember it: EXQUISITE. A sweetness I'd never gotten out of those beans, an odd smoothness, not a trace of bitterness. One of those cups you just stare at, as if asking it who are you and where did you come from.

I have hypotheses, because the frame lets me have them: cooler water extracts differently, punishes less; the coffee, with half an hour of air, had already released gas that might have been getting in the way. But they're half-hypotheses, because half of that cup happened without a witness. I don't know the exact water temperature when I came back. I don't know exactly how many minutes went by. I didn't measure anything, because I didn't know something worth measuring was happening. And that's the cruel part of the gift: that cup, the best surprise this hobby has ever given me, I can't repeat. It has no recipe. It slipped out of the archive, and what doesn't get recorded doesn't come back either: there's no road back to it. The law of irreversibility doesn't only apply to what gets ruined. The good stuff too, if it happens without a frame, ends up on the other side of a door that won't open again.

And in that cup there was a second trap, more dangerous than nostalgia: the temptation to believe my own hype. Because reward hijacks too. Rage at least shows up looking dangerous, so you go on guard by yourself; a hot streak shows up with music, and you don't distrust whatever comes to congratulate you. After that cup I spent a good while flirting with the idea that I'd discovered something, a shortcut, a hidden trick, when all I'd discovered was that chance knows how to make coffee too. I saw that for years at another scale, in crypto, where the person who does well once is almost never brought down by the fall: they're brought down by the rise, which switches their brain off right before the big bet. Euphoria makes decisions as irreversible as rage. Rage just gives you a warning.

The second cup was the opposite of the accident: it had an audience, and it was a disaster.

One day I had to make coffee for my coffee friends. The real ones, the ones who weigh and measure like I do, the ones who'd notice anything because noticing is the hobby we share. And that day, after years of practice, everything went wrong. Everything. The grind was off, my pours came out fast, the water ran through as if the coffee weren't there: underextracted, a thin, watery cup, the kind that tastes like the shadow of what it should have been. And the strangest part is that I saw it coming in real time, because the seismograph works even when the earthquake is you: I knew exactly what was failing while it failed, the rushed stream, the badly set grinder, and I didn't grab the wheel in time. And do you know why my pours came out fast? Because I was in a hurry to show off. My attention, which on ordinary mornings is entirely on the water, went to the audience. I was making the coffee to be applauded, not to make it come out right, and coffee doesn't negotiate with vanity. The twelve-millisecond animal doesn't only wake up for betrayals and scams. It also wakes up with ten eyes watching, wanting something as old as the scene at the party: to look good. And it pushes your hands just the same.

Years of practice and I failed at the basics, in front of the one audience that knew how to read the failure. That day I understood you don't graduate from this. Perception is a muscle, and muscles don't hand out diplomas: they respond when you train them and go slack exactly when you start looking at the audience instead of the bar. You beat the hard morning, alone, in your kitchen, and lose the one with the applause. And so on with everything.

### V. Enjoying the unenjoyable

Now, training perception has a reputation it only half deserves: that it turns people insufferable.

You know the classic result. The taster who can't drink a street-corner coffee anymore without a face like a funeral. The audiophile who doesn't listen to music anymore: he listens to flaws. The film buff who doesn't watch movies: he audits them. Training your taste, most of the time, is a straight road to enjoying fewer and fewer things with more and more arguments. Sharpened perception as a factory of disgust: the more you know, the less of the world tastes good. If that's the destination, it's a terrible deal. Nobody should train for years only to outgrow the country they live in.

At home, my mom made the coffee. A French press and a pitiful grinder. That's the word; I can't find a fairer one. One of those little grinders that don't grind so much as massacre, leaving the beans in pieces of every size, sand mixed with gravel. The coffee came out however it came out: thick, with sediment at the bottom of the cup, stronger some days than others without anyone knowing why. That was the coffee of my childhood, in the country that for decades exported the good beans and drank the bad ones, so used to that arrangement we didn't even notice it. I grew up with that taste in the background and it never would have occurred to me to question it.

The Daniel with the scale has two ways of going back to that cup. One is the funeral-faced taster's: disastrous grind, overextraction, unmeasured water, an indefensible cup. All true, and it says nothing. The other way is to read it whole. Because with the frame I have now, I can see what I couldn't see before: that this was a ritual too. My mom didn't have a scale, didn't have a thermometer, had no idea how many grams went into that press. And yet the coffee was there every morning, made by the same hands, at the same time. She didn't have the variables. She had the appointment. Which, after so many years of the hobby, is the only thing I have too. All my gear, all my numbers, all my precision, are just my way of doing what she already did with a grinder that didn't work: showing up.

That's what I mean by enjoying the unenjoyable, and it isn't resignation or pandering to the palate. A bad cup doesn't become delicious through goodwill. It becomes readable, which is something else. And what's readable can be lived in. A roadside coffee, read whole, carries the road inside it. The watery coffee of some random hotel tells me more about the hotel than the front desk does. And the French press from home, read with the palate I have today, doesn't taste like a mistake. It tastes like the first version of something I'm only now learning to do with numbers: showing up.

### VI. The world doesn't come with a scale

There's a Sherlock Holmes scene: Holmes asks Watson how many steps lead up to their rooms. Watson doesn't know. He's climbed them hundreds of times, he lives there, and he doesn't know. Seventeen, Holmes tells him, and hands him the whole diagnosis in one sentence: you see, but you do not observe. Watson's eyes were fine. What he didn't have was the frame.

Now I'll ask you, no tricks: how many steps are on the staircase at your place, the one you climb every day? What color are the eyes of the person who greets you every morning? What did you have for lunch the day before yesterday? It isn't a memory game. It's an inventory of everything that passed in front of you without passing through you.

Science put numbers on that embarrassment. At Harvard they had people watch a video and count a basketball team's passes. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walks through the middle of the court, beats their chest, and walks off. Half the people don't see it. It isn't that they see it and forget: they don't see it. Their eyes registered it, their attention was busy counting, and the gorilla passed through the world without passing through anyone. That's what we are most of the day: people counting passes while the gorilla crosses the room.

And the day is full of gorillas, just smaller and more important ones. The voice of someone you love arrives half a degree colder than yesterday, and that half degree is all the information there is, and half the time it goes by like the gorilla. The "all good" that wasn't. The exhaustion your body was warning you about in tiny signals, the strange sleep, the tense shoulder, weeks before the crash we later call sudden. The street you walk every day that today has a blind drawn that was always open. None of that comes with a scale. Nobody weighs the conversation before serving it to you. Out there, every variable goes unrecorded, and all of life is a coffee forgotten halfway through: something worth measuring is happening, and you don't even know it's happening.

A Frenchman took the exercise all the way. Georges Perec sat for three days in Place Saint-Sulpice in Paris writing down everything that doesn't count: the buses going by, the pigeons all taking off at once, people carrying packages, umbrellas. Nothing more. He gave it a name I love: the infra-ordinary. What happens when nothing happens. A man archiving what the rest of the world throws away unopened. I read that and I don't see some odd literary experiment. I see a colleague.

The irony in my case is that this is literally my job, just in reverse. I train machines to detect on a call what a distracted ear misses: the intention behind the sentence, the temperature of a voice, the customer who says yes but sounds like no. Perception software, you could call it. And the human version of that software, mine, gets calibrated every morning in a kitchen, with a scale and hot water, reading the one signal I'm guaranteed before I go out to read all the others.

---

### VII. The tuning

Someday someone will ask me again why I don't buy one of those machines that make coffee by themselves. And I already know I'll answer with some nonsense, because the real answer doesn't fit in a hallway conversation. But it fits here. The machine is still better than me at everything measurable: more consistent, more precise, more stable. What it can't do is the one thing coffee is for, for me. Because I understood a long time ago that those ten minutes don't buy a better cup. They calibrate the instrument. A musician doesn't tune the guitar because tuning sounds nice; they tune it for everything that comes after. Coffee isn't the concert. It's the tuning.

And tomorrow I could blow it. After all these years and thousands of cups, tomorrow I could make a mediocre coffee, and I defend that possibility, because the day I can no longer ruin a cup is the day I'm no longer there. There'll be coffee. There won't be anyone tuning.

You have one of these too. It's almost certainly not coffee. It's that thing you do every day that could already do itself, and that for some reason you can't explain you keep doing with your hands. That's your scale, even if it doesn't weigh anything. What it trains isn't the thing. It's the appointment.

Mine is already poured. I drink my coffee and head out, into a day that doesn't repeat, can't be corrected, and won't be given back, full of uncounted steps and gorillas crossing rooms and voices half a degree colder, to see how many signals I can read before they pass. The tuning is over.

The concert is out there.
