# Permission to Stop

*On the stillness you don't choose, the noise you do, and what's left when you switch everything off*

He described himself as a *recovering scientist*. He'd say it with the half-smile of someone who knows he's lying. Because that guy hadn't recovered from anything. He was a scientist to the bone. You could see it in the way he listened: he didn't nod out of politeness, he nodded because he was processing. You could see it in the way he spoke: he had a particular cadence, a way of building sentences that made you want to stay and listen even if you had no business being there. A magnetism that wasn't the kind you build or rehearse. It was the kind that just is, like an accent or the way someone walks. When he talked, the room settled in to listen. Just like that, without his asking.

He was the kind of leader who works as a shield for his team. Not in an epic or visible way. In a quiet way. The kind his people probably never fully grasped, because that's the whole point of a good shield: the people behind it don't feel the impact. He stood between his team and the noise outside. He absorbed the pressure so they could think clearly. He gave them room to do their work without having to navigate the politics, the manufactured urgencies, the corporate theater that distracts more than it contributes. And he did it without making a show of it. It wasn't his personal brand. That's just how he was. There were people on his team who probably never knew how many hard conversations he had so they wouldn't have to. How many times he put himself in the middle of something so the impact wouldn't reach them. That's real leadership. Not the kind that announces itself. The kind you feel in its absence.

He worked in oncology. He'd dedicated his life to fighting cancer. To designing strategies, thinking about patients, moving the pieces people don't see but that hold up everything between a diagnosis and a treatment. And cancer took him. In a month. One month from diagnosis to gone. The cruelest irony there is: that the thing you fight is the thing that puts you out. That the disease you devoted your career to beating doesn't even give you time to fight your own fight.

He stopped joining calls shortly after the diagnosis. But he was still present. We thought about him constantly. In every meeting there was a space where he should have been, and we all felt it without saying it. We were finishing dividing up his responsibilities. Not as an administrative chore. As something nobody wanted to do, because divvying up someone's work is accepting that they won't be doing it again. Every task reassigned to someone else was a silent way of saying goodbye. Nobody said it that way, but we all knew.

He died on Thursday. I found out Saturday afternoon. Two days later. Two days in which I kept living as if nothing had happened, working, answering messages, doing what I always do. And that creates a particular helplessness: knowing something already happened and you kept functioning as if the world hadn't changed. That while you were reading emails on a Friday, someone you knew was already gone. And you didn't know. The world doesn't warn you. It doesn't stop for a second to say "hey, something changed, adjust." It keeps turning and you keep going inside it, doing the same old thing, without finding out something broke until someone tells you in a text on a Saturday afternoon.

---

### I. The days that didn't work

On Monday there was an executive call. You could feel a tension that wasn't the usual kind. Not the tension of deadlines or numbers that don't add up. Something else. The presence of an absence everyone recognized and nobody quite knew how to handle. We talked about what we had to talk about. But the air was different. The pauses were longer. The words were more measured. There was something not being said that we were all carrying, as if saying it out loud would mean accepting it in a way we weren't ready to yet.

On Tuesday we held a memorial for him. It was beautiful. Not in the aesthetic sense. In the sense of seeing that a person mattered as much to other people as he did to you. That what you felt wasn't a lonely grief but something shared. That the cadence, the magnetism, the shield, all the things I saw, others saw too. People told stories I didn't know. Details of conversations he'd had with each of them. Moments when he was there without anyone asking. The accumulation of small gestures that seem like little on their own and together draw a whole person. And that brings calm. A strange calm, because it comes mixed with sadness, but calm all the same. Knowing a person left that kind of mark on so many people is the clearest proof that he lived the way you're supposed to. That what he did mattered. That who he was mattered more.

After the memorial I tried to work. I sat down at the computer, opened the calendar, checked email. Everything was there. The meetings, the deadlines, the to-dos multiplying like rabbits. The machine was still running. I wasn't.

It wasn't sadness exactly. It was something more diffuse. As if the signal between my brain and my hands had gotten weak. I looked at the screen and the words didn't make sense. Not the words in the emails. Words in general. The idea that Tuesday mattered. The idea that anything on that screen was truly urgent. Everything looked the same as the week before and at the same time everything looked different, like when you change the lens on a camera and the same image goes out of focus without anything moving.

I turned off the monitor. Turned it back on an hour later. Turned it off again. I went to the kitchen, made coffee, sat in another chair, in another room, with the laptop closed beside me and the window in front of me. I drank the coffee looking out the window without thinking about anything in particular, which for someone who's always thinking about something in particular is an almost alien experience. The coffee went cold. I made another. That one went cold too. I didn't move from the chair. I didn't put on music. I didn't open my phone. I just sat there with cold coffee in my hands and a window that wasn't showing me anything in particular. And for the first time in a long time, I didn't feel the need to do anything about it.

What was happening wasn't that I couldn't work. It was that my body had decided it wasn't going to work and my head hadn't found out yet. I think of Cleo, in *Roma*. Cleo never stops. From the first scene she's working. She cleans, she looks after the children, she cooks, she does the laundry. And while she works, life runs her over. An earthquake. A boyfriend who leaves her. A loss. And she keeps going. She keeps functioning. Not because she's strong in the heroic sense of the word. Because she hasn't given herself permission not to. Until the scene at the sea forces her to stop. And when she stops, when she finally goes still, everything she'd been carrying behind a person who never stopped comes out. One sentence. Short. Devastating. Months of accumulation pouring out of a crack that could no longer be patched.

I didn't have a scene at the sea. I had a coffee that went cold twice and a monitor I turned on and off without writing anything. But the mechanics are the same. You keep functioning until you can't. And when you can't anymore, what comes out isn't that week's exhaustion. It's the exhaustion of every week. All at once. Out of nowhere. As if your body had been keeping a tab of every night you didn't sleep, every Sunday you worked, every vacation you didn't take, and one day decided to hand you the full bill without warning.

### II. Three years. Or eight.

I've gone three years without stopping. Not as a figure of speech. Three real, measurable years without taking a single day completely off. The closest I came was eight days away. And on the fourth day I found Wi-Fi and started reading email. Meaning: not even those eight days were eight days. They were four. And then the permission ran out. If I'm honest, the last time I really stopped was almost eight years ago.

Eight years. I write it and it seems absurd. But when you live it one day at a time, every day has its justification. This email really was important. This meeting couldn't be moved. This project needs me. This essay wants to come out this week. And none of those justifications is a lie. They're all true. The problem is they're all true every day. And when every day is urgent, none of them is the day to stop. There's always a Monday with something that can't wait. There's always a Wednesday with an opportunity you'll lose if you don't grab it. And "I'll stop next week" becomes "next month for sure," which becomes eight years. Just like that, without noticing. Eight years go by doing "next week."

There's always something. An email to check. An idea to jot down. A message to answer on Google Chat. An essay to write at two in the morning. Always. Not because anyone makes me. Because that's how I work. Because the guy who takes care of things doesn't switch off. Because the machine always has something pending, and switching off the machine feels like deserting your post.

In this series I've talked about building your own reality, about taking care of things, about expecting nothing from anyone. And all of that is still true. But there's something I didn't say in any of those essays, not out of dishonesty but because I hadn't seen it yet: the same philosophy that holds you up can become the perfect excuse never to stop.

"I'll take care of it" is a position of strength. Until it isn't. Until "I'll take care of it" turns into "I'll take care of everything, all the time, no exceptions, no pause, no permission to not take care of anything for a while." And then what was a philosophy becomes inertia. And the inertia dresses up as purpose. I've seen it in myself. I see it every week. The inability to tell "I want to do this" from "I can't not do this." They feel the same inside. They have the same urgency. But one is a choice and the other is an addiction. And when you've gone eight years without stopping, the line between them was erased a long time ago.

I've been the guy who answers emails at eleven at night. The one with his phone on the nightstand, notifications on. The one who opens Google Chat before he opens his eyes. The one who turned "I'm intense about what matters to me" into an identity with no off switch. And every time someone told me "hey, get some rest," my answer, unspoken but felt, was always the same: this is what I do. This is who I am. Stopping is for people who don't have as much on the line.

That's a lie. And I know it because when my body stopped for me, when after that Saturday my head stopped working the way it always works, nothing fell apart. The emails kept coming. The meetings got rescheduled. The world kept turning with the same indifference it always turns with. And that, which should have been a relief, was the most uncomfortable part of all. Because if nothing falls apart when you stop, then all the time you didn't stop, all the cost you paid for not stopping, was for something that wasn't necessity. It was habit. Fear. An identity built around always being in motion.

I don't know which of the three is worse.

What I do know is that the death of someone who devoted his life to fighting the thing that killed him didn't "teach me to value time." That's the LinkedIn version of something much more uncomfortable. What it did was expose something that was already there: that I've spent years living as if stopping were a weakness, when stopping is the one thing I've never had the balls to do.

I start companies, I write, I lead, I give my opinion, I put myself out there. All of that takes courage of some kind. But sitting down on a Tuesday at ten in the morning to do absolutely nothing, without guilt, without my phone, without the voice in my head saying "you could be making progress on something"? That I haven't been able to do. That's what I'm missing. And it took someone leaving for me to see it.

And there's something else I keep turning over. He worked in oncology. I work in oncology. We're on the same side of that trench. And when someone on your side falls to the very thing you're fighting, the question "why am I running so hard?" stops being philosophical and gets very concrete. Because if the disease doesn't distinguish between the people fighting it and the people who aren't, if a month is all it gives you no matter how much you gave it, then the only thing you really control is what you do with the days you do have. And what I was doing with mine was not stopping. Running. Producing. Optimizing. Answering the next email. Closing the next deal. Writing the next essay. Always the next. Never this one.

### III. What can't be rated

John Green wrote a book called *The Anthropocene Reviewed*, in which he rates human experiences with stars. Like Google reviews, but for things that don't get reviewed: our capacity for wonder, sunsets, the Piggly Wiggly, the plague. Green gives stars to the experience of being human with the same seriousness someone rates a restaurant. And what looks like an ironic exercise ends up being something more: a way of saying we live obsessed with measuring, rating, ranking, optimizing every aspect of existence. That we turn everything into a metric. And that the things that matter most are exactly the ones that don't fit on a scale of one to five.

I do that with everything that isn't fleeting. With work, with things that take time and repetition, with systems that can be improved. I measure. I optimize. I look for the angle with the best return. I treat every problem as a system that can be iterated. If something can be made more efficient, I make it more efficient. If a process can be automated, I automate it. If a decision can be backed by data, I back it with data. And that works. For a lot of things it works incredibly well. It's what lets me handle several industries at once, write essays at dawn and still function the next day, have the life I have. The optimizer brain is a powerful tool. The problem is when it becomes the only tool you have. When everything passes through that filter. When you try to process even a week of grief like a sprint you have to close.

But grief doesn't optimize. Loss has no KPI. Friendship isn't measured in deliverables or impact or billable hours. And when I tried to process that Saturday with the same tools I use for everything else (analyze, understand, extract the lesson, move on), the tools didn't work. Not because they were broken. Because they were the wrong tools. It's like trying to weigh a silence on a scale. The scale works perfectly. Silences just can't be weighed.

Green says that when he was at his worst with anxiety, what helped most wasn't a technique, a framework, an optimized wellness system. It was giving up on rating his experience. Giving up on asking whether what he felt was reasonable, proportionate, justified. Just feeling it. No grade. No stars. No urge to turn it into something productive. There's a moment in the book where Green gives sunsets five stars. Five stars. No justification, no analysis, no breakdown. And that, coming from a guy whose job is analyzing and breaking things down, is an act of surrender. Of accepting that some things simply are, that they don't need your opinion to exist, that they don't get better because you rate them.

I don't know how to do that. Or I didn't. Because this week, without meaning to, I did it. I sat with what I was feeling without trying to fix it. Without trying to pull a lesson from it. Without trying to turn what happened into content, into reflection, into something that would "be useful for something." And what I found was a strange stillness. Uncomfortable. The kind I'm not used to feeling, because my operating system is designed to move, not to stay. A stillness that produces nothing. That isn't headed anywhere. That just is. And for the first time, it didn't make me anxious. It gave me something like rest. Not the rest of sleeping eight hours. Another kind. The rest of no longer trying.

What someone's death puts in front of you isn't the question of whether you're making the most of your time. That question is easy and the answer is always "I could do better," which is useless. The question it puts in front of you is simpler and therefore harder: are you enough? Not enough in productivity. Not enough in achievements. Enough as a person. Enough as a friend. Enough as someone who was there. Is what already exists of you, today, without doing anything more, without optimizing anything more, without adding another line to the résumé or closing another deal or writing another essay, enough?

He didn't define himself by what he produced. He defined himself by how he was with his people. By that cadence when he spoke that invited you to stay. By the invisible shield he held up for his team. By the curiosity he never lost, even while calling himself *recovering*. Nobody at his memorial talked about KPIs. They talked about how he made them feel. About what it was like to be in a room with him. About that feeling that when he listened to you, you were really being heard. Not the polite listening of someone waiting for their turn to talk. Real listening. The kind that registers, that retains, that later shows up in a gesture you didn't expect. The kind of listening I described in another essay in this series as the highest way of saying "you matter to me."

And that has no stars. No rating. It can't be measured. It's just felt. And when it goes, what's left isn't a productivity gap. It's a presence gap. Which is infinitely bigger. Because productivity gets replaced. The tasks get divided up, the projects get redistributed, the spot on the org chart gets filled. But a person's presence, the specific way that person made you feel heard, made you feel what you said mattered, made you feel there was someone on the other side who didn't just hear but understood, that doesn't get divided up. That gets missed. Period.

### IV. Permission granted

Next week I'm taking a few days. Really off. Not "off but checking email." Not "off but message me if something urgent comes up." Off. Phone on mute. Chat closed. No essays. No pipelines. No metrics. No voice saying I should be doing something.

It's the first time in three years. Or eight, if we're being honest.

I'm not doing it because what happened "inspired" me. I'm doing it because I realized I'd gone years without giving myself permission and that I was waiting for something outside me to give it. That I needed something big enough to happen for the voice saying "keep going, keep going, keep going" to shut up for a while. And that's a problem. Because permission to stop shouldn't require a tragedy. It should be built into the design, not added as an emergency repair.

I don't know what I'll do with those days. Probably nothing. And that's the idea. To do nothing. Which sounds simple until you try it as someone for whom inactivity feels like an allergy. Which sounds like rest until you realize what's hard isn't stopping the body but stopping the head. That the head keeps making lists, projects, ideas, next steps, even when the body has already decided it isn't moving. By the second day I'll probably already be thinking about what to write when I'm back. By the third, my hands will probably be itching to open something, check something, move something. I know myself. I know I'll fight myself. I know the head will want to beat the body the way it always does. And the challenge won't be being off. The challenge will be letting being off be enough.

He would have understood this. Because he understood something I'm only beginning to see: that being present isn't a skill you practice in your free time. It's the skill. The only one that matters when everything else is stripped away. When the titles go, the projects, the metrics, the emails, when all of that goes, what's left is whether you were there or not. With your people. With yourself. In the moment that was happening while you were thinking about the next one.

I'm not going to close this essay with a pretty reflection about life. It's not what I need and it's not what you need to read. What I'll say is simpler: if you're reading this and you've gone months, or years, without really stopping, without switching everything off, without giving yourself permission to be unproductive for a while, don't wait for something to force you. Don't wait for the Saturday that changes your week. Don't wait for your body to stop for you, because when your body stops for you, it's already too late for it to be your decision.

Nobody gives you permission. Not the company, not the team, not the market, not the loss of someone you cared about. You give it to yourself. And giving it to yourself isn't weakness. It's the first honest decision you've made in a long time.

I'm going to switch everything off. For a few days. And when I'm back, I'm back. But for now, the coffee's gone cold, the laptop's closing, and what's left in this room is me. Doing nothing. And that, for the first time in a long time, is okay.
