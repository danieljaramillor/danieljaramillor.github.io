# Two Years Ago Today

*I lost (almost) everything*

Two years ago to the day, I flew to Germany. Those weeks were the most intense, most defining of my life.

It didn't start out of nowhere. It started months earlier, with an unexpected twist of fate. A friend reached out right after I sold RXXO. He was looking for someone to lead product on a chatbot he was building. "It's hourly, not a full-time job yet," he said. My days were free, given over to thinking and making things, so I said yes. It was a way to earn some extra money while I learned more about AI.

The chatbot's goal was simple but ambitious: fully anonymize personal information inside AI models. Let any business replace its support team in hours, not weeks. ChatGPT had been on the market for only a few months. It was the right time, the right place, the right opportunity. A couple of conversations later, I joined the team.

I started out leading three people. I fell in love with the idea, with the market. I caught that explosive energy you only feel when you're building something from scratch, when you're convinced you're about to change the world.

Sleep became optional.

Eighteen-hour days became the norm. Weekends belonged to the laptop. I started learning German. Every day, sketches turned into code, to-do lists got shorter, and the calls with Lenny were always a shot of adrenaline. We built without stopping.

Everything felt possible.

On one of those nights of deep ideation, thousands of words of diagrams and the blue glow of my bedroom light still on at 3 a.m., Lenny confessed that we had to start using encrypted messaging, that he felt the competition was watching us. "I don't trust the AI note-takers either," he said, eyes wide, sweeping the office with his stare. At the time, I chalked it up to stress and exhaustion. I laughed and told him he was being paranoid.

He didn't laugh.

The energy kept building. One day Lenny called me. He was impressed with my work and wanted me to come in as a kind of co-founder. He offered me the CPO role, with a salary and an equity package. I thought about it and countered: I'd take less salary, but I'd buy an additional 5% of the company. I wanted a more direct stake in the outcome.

Lenny agreed. He told me he had personally invested 2.5 million euros, and that I would buy my shares at that same valuation. On that call, we shook on it. I would go to Germany so that, in under three months, we'd have a solid team and a working product.

I put two conditions on the table: I needed to see the current financial statements to confirm the money existed, and I needed a clear budget for my team. He assured me everything was in order and gave me the green light to hire almost 45 people.

I said yes. And the world went into fast-forward.

I landed in Stuttgart a little after midnight. The cold October air hit me as I walked out of the airport. Lenny was waiting for me with his best friend, Marwand, our CTO and co-founder. We grabbed a quick dinner. Between the adrenaline of the trip and a couple of drinks, the conversation was a blur of plans and promises. "We can get your residency sorted," he said. "You'll stay in an apartment I'm about to have free." They dropped me at my hotel and we agreed to meet the next day for the drive to Hamburg.

The next afternoon, they picked me up in a screaming-green Lamborghini Urus, a 656-horsepower car I heard minutes before I saw. We drove all night. The jet lag turned every blink into a fight with the dark. I curled up in the back seat and tried to sleep while Lenny, grinning like a maniac, pushed the car to 230 kilometers an hour on the Autobahn. The highway lights were just a smeared line through the window.

We reached Hamburg at first light. There was no time to rest. We went straight to what would be our new office. Inside, we met Carol and Joanna, our fourth partners. Behind them, a full photo and video crew was waiting. Our welcome to the company was a photo shoot for our official profiles and the filming of our first promo video.

The place was stunning. Minimalist, polished concrete, enormous windows. An austere, brutally beautiful space. The flashes were blinding me and a director was shouting instructions. It didn't take us long to desecrate that temple of design. As soon as the team started arriving, we turned the office into our own private chaos. The immaculate boardroom drowned in a sea of monitors and Post-its. We took the Red Dot Design awards down from their pedestals to make room for more computers and for mountains of Red Bull cans that, stacked up, would have reached the third floor. Out on the terrace, a planter became a communal ashtray that was overflowing with butts in under two weeks.

The energy was electric. The tension, palpable. Anyone who walked into that office knew we were building something monumental. We had the arrogant certainty that we were the best and that we were going to change the world. Part of that whirlwind was hiring my friends. Some of them are probably reading this right now, remembering those video calls. Remembering that the person on the other side of the screen wasn't really me, but a version of me with a thousand-yard stare, completely burned out, running on nothing but the synthetic energy of cigarettes and caffeine.

In the middle of all that madness, I made a conscious effort to escape every night. Going back to the hotel, sleeping in an actual bed, was my anchor to reality. A way to reset and remind myself that days had an end. Lenny was the only one who broke that rule. Sometimes he'd stay in the office three days straight without leaving. His room reeked, but hey, that was "startup energy," right? The sacrifice it takes to become the next unicorn.

And honestly, we were pulling it off. Every morning I asked myself the same question: how could I do in days what I'd dreamed would take months and knew would take years? And every day, I proved to myself that it was possible. In one afternoon I built an onboarding flow and fully automated customer billing. In a single day I wrote a complete manual to train my team. In two days I designed the whole system for customers to feed their chatbot's knowledge base. The brain of our AI, the architecture Marwand and I had co-designed, worked flawlessly. Our developers, who were unbelievably talented, were assembling it faster than I had ever imagined.

There were no shortcuts, because everything we did was intentional. Every corner we cut to save time or compute was justified. We were going to change the world, right?

The days were brutal. One night I needed to get out. To escape the stale air and the artificial light, even for an hour, in the Urus or the Ferrari, I didn't care which. Lenny was asleep in some corner, so I went to look for the keys in his suitcase, which he'd left lying in a storage room.

I unzipped it. The smell of damp clothes hit me. I reached in, feeling for the metal of the keys, but my fingers bumped into something else. A Ziploc bag, bulging and heavy. I pulled it out. It was full of white powder.

My blood ran cold. The world stopped.

I looked at the door. I took out my phone, snapped a photo, and put the bag back in the suitcase, burying it under the dirty laundry. I zipped it shut and walked out, trying to keep my footsteps quiet. My brain was putting out nothing but static.

What the hell was going on?

The next morning, the emails started. A developer on my team wrote to me. "Dani, I haven't been paid." I asked him to forward me the invoice so I could escalate it with Laura, the accountant. A few minutes later, two more people wrote. Same story.

The next day, a past-due notice from AWS. Same process. Then one from Slack. And another from Proxify, the company that managed our most senior developers.

The total debt that day was €175,000. Not counting our own salaries.

That's when I felt the floor drop out of my stomach. I walked into Lenny's office.

"I need access to the account to pay my team," I told him straight out. "They're three days late."

"We're switching banks," he said, without looking at me. "Tell them to give us a couple more days. If they're in a hurry, they can talk to me." The explanation was too simple. Too easy. And with the image of that bag of white powder burned into my mind, none of it added up.

I sounded the first silent alarm. I wrote to my team. I told them they could stop working until they got their money. I admitted that Lenny's explanation didn't convince me and that I was going to dig deeper. I asked them to keep quiet and act normal. I didn't want to start a panic until I was sure.

I didn't sleep that night.

I stared at the ceiling, and the hole in my stomach was impossible to ignore. Were the financial statements fake? Had we bought the office without him telling us? Why was switching banks taking so long?

One question led to the next, sinking me into a spiral of doubt and suspicion.

The next morning, eyes burning from lack of sleep, I made my first move. I called Laura, the accountant. Lenny's girlfriend. I went straight at it. I explained the unpaid invoices and the pressure from the team. I asked her for the most recent bank statements, a clear timeline for the bank switch, and any concrete date I could give people. I needed answers, not excuses.

That was the day I lost my appetite completely.

Laura got back to me the next day. She sent some incredibly suspicious Revolut statements. They showed an account opened just the day before and a pending transfer that, according to the document, was failing because the sending bank had a "claim order" on it for too large an amount. I had never seen anything like it. It was strange. Very strange.

I went to Marwand. I asked him when he'd last been paid. He shrugged. "No idea," he said. "I don't care about the money. Lenny covers most of our expenses. Besides, we've got equity. They'll pay us eventually."

His blind faith chilled me to the bone.

Then I found Carol and Joanna. They were worried. They told me Lenny had been bouncing their invoices for weeks. Always a new excuse: "the format is wrong," "my bank can't process them right now," "I can only pay part of it, the full invoice is too much."

The pieces were starting to fit, and they made a terrifying picture.

I made a WhatsApp group with everyone I had hired. We started trading information quietly. The story was the same for everyone. Promises, delays, excuses.

With that ammunition, I confronted Lenny again. This time there would be no subtlety. I walked into his office.

"We're ten days late with the entire team," I told him. "They deserve an explanation. I'm setting up a meeting with everyone so you can face them... I've told them to stop working until you pay them."

By then I hadn't eaten in three days. It was stupid, I know. But my body was in survival mode. I did some quick math: I needed at least 1,500 calories to function. A carton of chocolate milk had 3,000. Simple math. I'd drink half every morning and call it a day. Water and cigarettes would do the rest. Who needs sleep anyway?

I had also canceled my hotel booking. I didn't know if I had any money. I didn't know if we'd lost the company's money. Every cent I could save was a cent for whatever came next. I started sleeping at the office.

Sometimes I called my friends. I didn't know what to say. "I feel lonely" was all I could manage to mumble. Maybe I didn't have the courage to admit that something was wrong. Or maybe I didn't want to scare them. My mind was in combat mode: contain the chaos, nothing else.

What came next is a blur. Everything piled up over two weeks that felt like a lifetime.

I called the banks. The ones Laura had mentioned, the ones I'd seen Lenny use. I introduced myself as a shareholder running an internal investigation. Only two confirmed we had accounts with them. The combined balance was 305 euros.

Three hundred and five euros. Nowhere near the two and a half million Lenny swore he'd invested.

But the hardest blow was something else. The account where I'd deposited the money for my shares was empty. It turned out we had used my investment to pay the first salaries and buy the new monitors. My desk. My chair.

I started holding secret calls with the team at night. We met in the back alley, between the trash and the cold, anywhere Lenny couldn't hear us. His paranoia grew at the same pace as our desperation. The emergency meetings I'd called became daily, a circus of ever more pathetic excuses on his part. The team started confronting him directly.

And then, on one call, a team member called him a con man and started posting memes in the company Slack. The secret was out. It was obvious to everyone: we didn't have a single cent.

The whole thing collapsed. Slack told us every message would be deleted if we didn't pay. We lost access to AWS. We almost lost our email. But the developers had been smarter than that: each of them had downloaded a copy of the code to their own machines. We had a backup. The plan was to sell the software, however we could, to try to recover some of the money.

On those late-night calls, I asked anyone who urgently needed the money to reach out to me. Of the 40 people on the team, I paid 25 salaries out of my own pocket. People with families to feed, school tuition to pay, medical treatments for their parents.

The friends I had hired, the real ones, told me they didn't need the money. That the experience had been enough. That they knew the damage was done and that I was wrecked. They could feel it.

It became obvious in those weeks. I wasn't really there anymore. I was just an image of myself, a memory, what was left of a Daniel burned out and eaten alive by all that energy. It was the closest I've ever come to losing everything.

And to be honest, the money wasn't even the worst part.

The worst part was the conversations. The one-on-one calls with every person I had hired with so much passion. Explaining, heart wide open, that I had been scammed, that we had been scammed. Telling them I was so, so sorry, that I had been made to believe this was real and that I had passed that energy, that confidence, on to them.

I tried to explain as best I could. I had given them my word.

And my word had been built on lies. No, I had been lied to. But that is a very hard conversation to have with anyone.

Especially with someone who has kids.

The obvious question is why I didn't leave. Why I stayed in that hell once I knew the truth. The whole time, I played a double role. I became a double agent. To Lenny, I was still the co-founder, the ally pushing him to keep going, making him believe I was on his side. To the rest of the team, I was the one collecting evidence, the one trying to understand how big the disaster was, the one saving every fake invoice and every excuse-filled email to build a case against him.

That's when all the pieces fell into place. Lenny was doing about five grams of cocaine a day. That explained the persecution complex. That's why he slept at the office. That's why he was constantly shaking. That's why he sometimes disappeared to the hospital for "cortisone pills." That's why his skin was wrecked and his teeth were a mess. And I, in my innocence, had explained it all away: "He's a driven German, we're building a startup, we're going to change the world." It all made sense at the time.

I still wasn't eating. I still wasn't sleeping. And the team could tell. My friends were more and more worried. I had to plan my escape.

The plan was simple. I'd use the same excuse I'd used with my friends: "I'm really lonely, really exhausted. I'm going to take a short break, a weekend trip to London to see a friend." I bought the flights. I even bought a return flight to Stuttgart, not Hamburg, with the excuse of picking up some things at the old coworking space.

Lenny kindly offered to drive me to the airport. By then his excuses had gone off the rails. The bank was holding his money because of a lien; he was being investigated over his crypto gains; his crypto account was frozen too. The pile of lies was so tall we could all see right through it.

I had to make my escape believable to someone who breathed lies. I planned it all for a week. I left things on my desk at the office: my mouse, my microphones, my keyboard, a jacket, a charger, and a small bag of clean clothes. The story was that I was taking my dirty laundry to wash at my friend's place. That guy could not suspect I wasn't coming back.

When the day came, I got in the car with Lenny. The drive to the airport was dead silent. I think, deep down, he knew I wasn't coming back. That it was all an act. That I could see right through him.

I walked into the airport. While I was in the security line, I called one of my friends on the team, the most senior developer I've ever known. I explained everything in more detail than ever before. To this day, he doesn't speak to me. If I were him, I wouldn't speak to me either.

I was more nervous than I've ever been in my life. In that line, a thousand thoughts hit me at once. What if Lenny knew? What if he'd planted drugs in my bag? What if the dogs smelled it? What if he'd called the police to accuse me of some made-up crime and trap me in Germany? What if they wouldn't let me out at the border?

I handed my passport to the officer, expecting him at any moment to get on the radio and have me taken to a dark room. He stamped my passport and handed it back.

Freedom.

I boarded the flight from Hamburg to London Heathrow. It was the happiest flight of my life. I wanted to order a glass of wine, but I didn't want to spend the extra euros. When I landed in London, I told the immigration officer, in full detail, everything that had happened. I needed someone to listen; my head needed to start letting go. It was magic. He understood. He was glad it had turned out okay.

I took a cab to Martin's place.

I cried.

I took a shower, my first in five days. I felt hungry, for the first time in two weeks. I was ready to eat something that wasn't chocolate milk.

I was free. I had made it out alive, in one piece. And I cried again, but this time it wasn't from sadness.

The color came back to my face over the next few days. My friends back home and the employees I was still in touch with told me my voice sounded different, that I was back.

Together, we managed to find other victims, other companies and people Lenny owed money to. We sued him collectively. In total, the fraud came to more than 4 million euros.

Lenny went to prison. Sadly, he's already out for good behavior. He doesn't have a cent to pay me back, or anyone else. He's ruined. His family disowned him.

Knowing he's free doesn't make me happy. But it gives me some peace to know he will never get to live again. Really live.

There was a Daniel before Germany and there is a Daniel now. Some people say I've lived several lives in one, but I've only lived mine. And while I can't undo what was done, I can't unlearn what I learned either. All of it made me the person I am today.

So thank you, Lenny, you absolute son of a bitch. Thank you.

And you, Marwand, now starting your own startup: thank you too.

Thank you for teaching me in a few months what takes other people a lifetime: how little money is worth next to integrity. Thank you for showing me the limits of the body and the incredible resilience of the mind. Thank you for making me who I am. Thank you for teaching me that the only thing we can take for granted in life is the person we build every day, that life is meant to be shared and enjoyed. To fill it with people who are like vitamins, people who love you for who you really are.

I hope that someday, in your miserable lives, you get to feel, even for an instant, the happiness I feel every sunrise. That's the proof that I got out, that I'm free, and that I'm a better person than either of you will ever be.

P.S.

There's one more victim in this story I haven't talked about: Marwand's father. An immigrant from Afghanistan who worked for two decades at a McDonald's to provide for his family. Marwand, completely taken in by Lenny's smooth talk, convinced him to quit that 20-year job to come be our cook.

The result was predictable: when the house of cards fell, Marwand's whole family lost everything and had to move in with a relative in the Netherlands. Now he has a new startup, built, I suspect, on the foundations of our code.

I have only two things left to say. The first is a hope: that someday you learn what friendship really means. The second is a secret: I hope you never find out who quietly paid your parents' last three months of rent, before you left them out on the street.
